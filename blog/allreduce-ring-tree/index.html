The Cost of AllReduce Algorithms from Ring to Tree

This note derives the lower bound of allreduce and the cost of ring, recursive doubling, Rabenseifner and tree algorithms. With it, a reader can predict which algorithm wins for a given message size and rank count.

Allreduce sums a vector across all ranks and leaves the result on every rank, and data-parallel training runs one at the end of every step. This note records the cost model of allreduce and the algorithms that implement it: ring, recursive doubling, Rabenseifner’s reduce-scatter plus allgather, and trees. It states the lower bound, derives the time of each algorithm in a simple α–β model, and works through examples with 8, 64 and 512 ranks. It ends with what NCCL and in-network reduction change. The MPI rules that these algorithms rely on are recorded in What the MPI Standard Guarantees for Point-to-Point Messages, and the effect of the node boundary is analyzed in Hierarchical AllReduce and the Network-Card Bottleneck.

The notation is fixed here. There are NNN ranks, and each rank holds a vector of SSS bytes. The allreduce sums the NNN vectors element by element and leaves the SSS-byte result on every rank. α\alphaα is the latency of one message, and β\betaβ is the transfer time per byte, so the bandwidth is 1/β1/\beta1/β. The α–β model counts the bytes that each rank sends and receives; it does not describe how these bytes share the physical links, and the section on topology returns to this point. Numbers marked (computed) are my own arithmetic on the constants stated in the text; all other facts come from the sources listed at the end.

The lower bound

Every allreduce has to move about twice the data of one rank. An allreduce is a reduction followed by a distribution, and no algorithm can avoid moving the data twice in some form. The standard lower bound counts what each rank must send or receive: 2S(N−1)/N2S(N-1)/N2S(N−1)/N bytes for the bandwidth term, and at least ⌈log⁡2N⌉\lceil \log_2 N \rceil⌈log2​N⌉ rounds of messages for the latency term. In the α–β model, this gives

Tallreduce≥α⌈log⁡2N⌉+2S(N−1)Nβ.T_{\text{allreduce}} \ge \alpha \lceil \log_2 N \rceil + \frac{2S(N-1)}{N}\beta .Tallreduce​≥α⌈log2​N⌉+N2S(N−1)​β.

The bandwidth term is easy to read. The factor 2(N−1)/N2(N-1)/N2(N−1)/N approaches 2 as NNN grows, so each rank must move about twice its own data, however many ranks there are. The latency term says that the number of rounds can be logarithmic in NNN. A ring needs 2(N−1)2(N-1)2(N−1) rounds instead, and this is why it loses badly on small messages at large NNN.

No simple algorithm reaches both terms at once. The ring reaches the bandwidth term but not the latency term. Recursive doubling reaches the latency term but misses the bandwidth term by a factor of about log⁡2N/2\log_2 N / 2log2​N/2. Rabenseifner’s algorithm comes within a factor of two of the latency term and reaches the bandwidth term, and MPICH uses it for long messages for this reason.

Ring allreduce

The ring is the algorithm most people learn first, because its traffic is optimal and its picture is simple. The ranks form a cycle, and the vector is split into NNN chunks of S/NS/NS/N bytes each. The algorithm runs in two phases:

  • Reduce-scatter, N−1N-1N−1 steps. In each step, every rank sends the chunk it has just reduced to its right neighbor, receives the next chunk from its left neighbor, and adds the incoming chunk to its own copy. After N−1N-1N−1 steps, each rank holds the fully reduced version of exactly one chunk.
  • Allgather, N−1N-1N−1 steps. The ranks now pass the reduced chunks around the ring in the same way. Each rank forwards what it receives and keeps a copy, so after N−1N-1N−1 more steps every rank has all NNN chunks.

Both phases cost the same, so the total time is

Tring=2(N−1)(α+SNβ)=2(N−1)α+2S(N−1)Nβ.T_{\text{ring}} = 2(N-1)\left(\alpha + \frac{S}{N}\beta\right) = 2(N-1)\alpha + \frac{2S(N-1)}{N}\beta .Tring​=2(N−1)(α+NS​β)=2(N−1)α+N2S(N−1)​β.

The ring is bandwidth-optimal, and its latency grows linearly. The bandwidth term matches the lower bound exactly. The latency term is 2(N−1)α2(N-1)\alpha2(N−1)α, linear in NNN, and this is the whole problem. At 512 ranks, a ring needs 1,022 rounds of messages. At 1.5 µs per round, this is 1.5 ms before a single byte is counted (computed), and for an 8 KB message it is almost all of the time.

The ring has one more advantage in the model: every logical link of the ring carries the same amount of traffic. Whether the physical network stays free of congestion depends on how the ring maps onto it; if two links of the ring share a switch port or an uplink, that port carries twice the traffic. Its communication only between neighbors also maps well onto a torus or onto a ring of NVLink domains. The ring is therefore the right choice for large messages, where the bandwidth term dominates, and the wrong choice for small ones.

Recursive doubling

Recursive doubling trades bytes for rounds. In step iii, rank rrr exchanges its whole vector with rank r⊕2ir \oplus 2^ir⊕2i, and both ranks add the two vectors. After log⁡2N\log_2 Nlog2​N steps, every rank has the total. The algorithm assumes that NNN is a power of two. Its time is

Tdoubling=log⁡2N(α+Sβ).T_{\text{doubling}} = \log_2 N \left(\alpha + S\beta\right) .Tdoubling​=log2​N(α+Sβ).

The latency term reaches the logarithmic bound, and for small messages the latency term is all that matters. At 512 ranks and 8 KB, 9 rounds of about 2.2 µs each beat the 1,022 rounds of the ring (computed). The cost is bandwidth. Each step sends the whole vector, so each rank sends Slog⁡2NS \log_2 NSlog2​N bytes instead of about 2S2S2S. This is a factor of log⁡2N/2\log_2 N / 2log2​N/2 more, or 4.5 times more at 512 ranks for the bandwidth term alone. With 1 GB per rank, recursive doubling is therefore much slower than the ring.

This is the clearest example of the trade-off that runs through this note: the ring minimizes bytes and pays in rounds, while recursive doubling minimizes rounds and pays in bytes.

Rabenseifner’s algorithm

Rabenseifner’s algorithm gets both terms nearly right, because it halves the message in each step instead of sending the whole vector. It also runs in two phases:

  • Reduce-scatter by recursive halving. In each step, a rank exchanges half of its remaining data with a partner and adds what it receives. The pieces shrink geometrically, so each rank sends S(N−1)/NS(N-1)/NS(N−1)/N bytes in total, not Slog⁡2NS \log_2 NSlog2​N.
  • Allgather by recursive doubling. The reduced pieces are gathered back in log⁡2N\log_2 Nlog2​N steps. In each step, the part of the vector that a rank holds doubles, and the traffic again grows geometrically.

The total time is

TRabenseifner=2log⁡2N⋅α+2S(N−1)Nβ.T_{\text{Rabenseifner}} = 2\log_2 N \cdot \alpha + \frac{2S(N-1)}{N}\beta .TRabenseifner​=2log2​N⋅α+N2S(N−1)​β.

The bandwidth term meets the lower bound, and the latency term is twice the bound, because the algorithm runs two phases. The algorithm is as bandwidth-efficient as the ring and nearly as latency-efficient as recursive doubling. Its price is complexity in the implementation: buffers that are not contiguous, halving patterns, and a separate code path when NNN is not a power of two. For this reason, it is a library default and rarely written by hand.

Trees

A tree reduces toward a root and then broadcasts back. In a binary tree, every rank except the root sends its whole vector to its parent once in the reduce phase, and every internal node receives one vector from each child. The broadcast phase repeats this pattern in the other direction. Each phase takes about log⁡2N\log_2 Nlog2​N levels, so the latency is about 2log⁡2N2\log_2 N2log2​N rounds, close to the bound.

The bandwidth depends on pipelining. Without pipelining, each level must move the whole vector before the next level starts, so the bandwidth term is about 2log⁡2N⋅Sβ2\log_2 N \cdot S\beta2log2​N⋅Sβ (computed from the model). With pipelining, the vector is split into small chunks, and different levels work on different chunks at the same time, so the bandwidth term no longer grows with the number of levels. A single binary tree still uses its links unevenly. In the reduce phase, a leaf only sends and never receives, while an internal node receives from two children, so half of the receive bandwidth in the tree stays unused.

NCCL’s double binary tree removes this imbalance. The data is split between two binary trees. A rank that is a leaf in one tree is an internal node in the other, and no rank is an internal node in both trees. Each rank therefore does about the same amount of work, and the two trees together give full bandwidth for reduce and broadcast with logarithmic latency. NCCL 2.4 introduced this design for allreduce at large scale, where the linear latency of the ring becomes the limit.

Worked example: three message sizes (computed)

All numbers in this section are arithmetic on the formulas above. Take α=1.5\alpha = 1.5α=1.5 µs and an effective bandwidth of 12.5 GB/s per link, so β=0.08\beta = 0.08β=0.08 ns per byte, roughly a 100 Gb/s network. These are round numbers; the point is the shape of the results, not the constants.

Large messages, S=1S = 1S=1 GB per rank:

64158 ms158 ms480 ms
512161 ms160 ms720 ms

The ring and Rabenseifner’s algorithm are almost the same at this size, because the bandwidth term dominates and both algorithms reach it. Their difference in latency, 1.5 ms against 27 µs at 512 ranks, is about 1% of the total. Recursive doubling is 4.5 times slower than Rabenseifner at 512 ranks, exactly the factor log⁡2N/2\log_2 N / 2log2​N/2.

Small messages, S=8S = 8S=8 KB per rank:

RanksRingRabenseifnerRecursive doubling
822 µs10 µs6.5 µs
64190 µs19 µs13 µs
5121,534 µs28 µs19 µs

Here the order is completely reversed. The 1,022 rounds of the ring make it about 80 times slower than recursive doubling at 512 ranks. Rabenseifner’s algorithm stays within a factor of 1.5 of recursive doubling, because its latency term is 2log⁡2N2\log_2 N2log2​N instead of log⁡2N\log_2 Nlog2​N. These two tables are the argument for choosing the algorithm by message size, and MPI libraries and NCCL both do so.

Between these two sizes lies a third range. There, the message is large enough for bandwidth to matter and small enough for the number of rounds to matter too. In this range, Rabenseifner’s algorithm usually wins, because it is the only one of the three that does well on both terms.

Topology: why the constants are not constant

The α–β model treats the network as uniform, and real clusters are not. A fat tree offers full bisection bandwidth at the cost of many switches. A dragonfly uses switches with many ports and long global links. A GPU node has NVLink inside and InfiniBand or Ethernet between nodes. For a message that crosses tiers, the effective β\betaβ is that of the slowest link, and the effective α\alphaα includes the hops through the switches.

Two consequences follow. First, the placement of ranks matters. An allreduce over ranks spread across tiers moves more bytes over slow links than an allreduce over ranks packed into one node, so placing ranks according to the topology saves real time. Second, the best algorithm can depend on the topology as well as on the message size. A ring fits a torus naturally, a tree fits a fat tree naturally, and a ring over a fat tree with random placement wastes bisection bandwidth.

NCCL adds topology awareness and runs each collective in one kernel. It detects the topology, such as NVLink domains, PCIe switches and the InfiniBand fabric, and it orders its rings and trees to match. It runs each collective as a single CUDA kernel that handles both the communication and the reduction, instead of a sequence of copies and separate kernels launched from the host. It chooses an algorithm automatically for each call, based on the hardware and the topology, and the variable NCCL_ALGO overrides this choice. The algorithms are the ones in this note; the engineering lies in the mapping to the hardware and in removing host overhead from every step.

In-network reduction

SHARP moves the reduction into the switches. Instead of sending its data to a peer that adds it, each rank sends its data to a switch. The switches form an aggregation tree, add the data in their buffers, and send only the reduced result back. Each endpoint then sends its data once and receives the result once, so the cost no longer follows the α–β model of the host-based algorithms in this note.

The condition is hardware support at three levels: switches that support SHARP, a fabric manager that configures the aggregation trees, and a communication library that uses them, such as NCCL with its CollNet support or an MPI library with SHARP support. Without all three, the library falls back to a ring or a tree. NCCL also offers NVLink SHARP (the NVLS algorithms) for reductions inside an NVLink domain.

What this means for training

The allreduce of gradients can cap the step rate. A data-parallel training step ends with an allreduce of the gradients, where SSS is the size of the gradients in bytes, and the step cannot finish before the allreduce does. At 64 ranks with 100 Gb/s links, a 1 GB allreduce takes about 158 ms according to the table above (computed). Communication alone then limits training to about 6.3 steps per second, before any computation. For this reason, large-model training uses three techniques: sharding the gradients with a reduce-scatter instead of a full allreduce, overlapping communication with the backward pass, and faster interconnects.

Overlap works as follows. An allreduce can start on a bucket of gradients as soon as the backward pass has produced that bucket, so the communication runs while the remaining computation continues. The lower bound does not change; what changes is whether its time is visible in the step time.
