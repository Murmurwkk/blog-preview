Supervising LLM Reasoning: From Chain of Thought to SCoRe

This note follows CoT, sampling with selection, process rewards, STaR, outcome-reward RL and SCoRe through one worked example. With it, a reader can tell what each method supervises and what it cannot see.

Several methods aim to make language models reason better: chain-of-thought prompting, sampling with selection, process reward models, training on the model’s own successes (STaR), outcome-reward reinforcement learning, and trained self-correction (SCoRe). They differ mainly in what they supervise and in which step of the model’s work they change. This note goes through the methods in the order in which the field developed them, and at each stage it asks what the method actually changes and what it leaves unsolved. All model results are the papers’ own, at the linked versions; I trained and sampled no language model, and my own checks are small numerical scripts whose numbers are marked (computed).

A running example: two solutions to one equation

Consider a one-line equation: 2(x−3)=102(x-3)=102(x−3)=10. Two students solve it.

Student A writes: “Divide by 2 to get x−3=5x-3=5x−3=5, so x=8x=8x=8.” Student B writes: “Expand to get 2x=142x=142x=14, so x=8x=8x=8.” Both final answers are correct. However, both middle steps of B are wrong, and the two errors happen to cancel out on the way back to 8.

A grader that only says “correct” cannot show which step it checked. This note runs these two solutions through every method that it discusses.

Once a model has produced a solution, several different things can be done with it, and the techniques that are all called “making models reason better” differ mainly in which of these things they do:

  • Sample more solutions and pick the most reliable one. This does not change the weights.
  • Select with an evaluator. This only changes which solution is submitted this time.
  • Train on the successes. This changes what the model generates next time.
  • Revise: let the model read what it just wrote and try again, conditioned on its own text.

Figure 1: A map of this post. The top half is one round of inference; training updates and second attempts happen in different places. A second attempt can read existing text but doesn’t change parameters by itself, and the feedback may be just a generic “check your work” instruction with no correct answer in it.

A method that helps at one step is not guaranteed to help at another step. The stages below follow this order: CoT (prompting), self-consistency and process reward models (sampling and selection), STaR and outcome-reward RL (training), and SCoRe (revision).

Three questions about one solution

Before any method is discussed, it helps to separate three different judgments about a solution:

  1. Does the answer satisfy the problem?
  2. Does the written derivation support the answer?
  3. Did the model actually compute along the reasoning in the text?

The first two questions can be checked, but the third cannot be checked from the text alone. Student A and Student B agree on question 1 and differ on question 2. Long solutions with trials and backtracking need one more distinction: a failed route that is explicitly abandoned is not the same failure as an error that remains in the final proof.

Stage 1: CoT changes the input, not the weights

An autoregressive model reads everything before token ttt when it generates this token, including what it has just written, so intermediate text becomes input to the later computation. The original CoT paper gave several examples of the form “question, reasoning, answer” and had the model imitate them on new questions, without fine-tuning any weights (CoT paper).

The form alone is not enough. The paper tried outputting only equations, padding the output with meaningless dots, and moving the explanation after the final answer, and on complex arithmetic none of these variants reliably reproduced the effect of normal CoT. So both the content of the intermediate text and its position matter.

The same paper also inspected the GSM8K answers of LaMDA 137B by hand: of 50 samples with correct final answers, 2 had flawed reasoning. The sample is small, but it is enough to show that checking only the answers lets invalid reasoning pass.

The visible generation can be written down. With the question xxx, the intermediate text zzz and the answer aaa, the model produces

(z,a)∼πθ(⋅∣x,p),(z,a)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,p),(z,a)∼πθ​(⋅∣x,p),

where ppp is the prompt with its examples. One sample is one draw. If the first draw misses the correct trajectory, there are two possibilities: either the model almost never produces this trajectory, or the correct trajectory has some probability and this draw simply missed it. These two situations call for different remedies.

Stage 2: sampling covers, selection chooses

Coverage

For a fixed question with a single-sample success probability ppp, the probability of at least one success in kkk samples is

pass@⁡k=1−(1−p)k.\operatorname{pass@}k=1-(1-p)^k.pass@k=1−(1−p)k.

This metric measures whether the candidate set covers a correct answer. It can be computed with the reference answer afterwards. A deployed system cannot do this, because it still has to choose one of its candidates to submit.

Take a simplified case with only two kinds of answer, right and wrong. For odd kkk, a majority vote picks the right answer with probability

Pvote=∑j=(k+1)/2k(kj)pj(1−p)k−j.P_{\mathrm{vote}} =\sum_{j=(k+1)/2}^{k}\binom{k}{j}p^j(1-p)^{k-j}.Pvote​=j=(k+1)/2∑k​(jk​)pj(1−p)k−j.

At p=0.4p=0.4p=0.4, more candidates make it more likely that at least one of them is right, but they also make the majority vote more reliably wrong. So “the correct answer appeared” and “the correct answer was chosen” are different events.

Figure 2: Computed from the binomial distribution with p=0.4p=0.4p=0.4 and 0.60.60.6; the fully correlated line copies the same answer every time. No language model was called. On open-ended math, wrong answers scatter across many values, so a correct answer under 50% can still win the plurality; real self-consistency also depends on answer normalization, tie handling and correlation between samples.

When nnn samples have been generated and ccc of them are correct, the coverage of kkk samples drawn uniformly from them is

pass@⁡k^=1−(n−ck)(nk),k≤n.\widehat{\operatorname{pass@}k} =1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom nk},\qquad k\le n.pass@k​=1−(kn​)(kn−c​)​,k≤n.

Using this value to estimate a fixed generation policy also requires a sampling assumption. Changing the prompt or the temperature, or letting round two read round one, does not produce interchangeable independent samples.

Selection

An evaluator handles the step after the candidates. When exactly one candidate may be submitted, the final success decomposes as

P(submit a correct answer)=P(a correct answer is among the candidates)P(choose correctly∣one is present).P(\text{submit a correct answer}) =P(\text{a correct answer is among the candidates}) P(\text{choose correctly}\mid\text{one is present}).P(submit a correct answer)=P(a correct answer is among the candidates)P(choose correctly∣one is present).

The second factor is conditional, so the two factors need not be independent. More sampling may raise the first factor while it brings in more wrong candidates that can mislead the evaluator, so the final success does not necessarily rise with it.

Let’s Verify Step by Step compares exactly this stage. With the generator fixed, it selects with an outcome reward model (ORM), a process reward model (PRM) or a majority vote. At best-of-1860 in Figure 3, the results are 78.2% for the PRM, 72.4% for the ORM and 69.6% for the majority vote. These results come from 1860 samples per question followed by one choice, so they are neither the accuracy of the first answer nor oracle coverage.

The data also matters. That experiment kept 500 problems from the original MATH test set for evaluation and added the remaining 4500 to the original 7500 training problems, and the base model and the generator also received extra math training. Under these conditions, process evaluation genuinely improves selection. What improved, however, is the choice among the candidates, while the generator itself stayed the same.

Turning step scores into one candidate score

The labeling unit of a PRM is the step, with positive, negative and neutral labels. Such labels can point to the place where an error starts, instead of judging a solution that is “all correct until the last calculation” as worthless. The experiments of the paper used filtered data of about 800k step labels, 75k solutions and 12k problems (the public, unfiltered release is larger).

Step labels are not yet a selection rule. With per-step scores qtq_tqt​ for “not an error”, in which positive and neutral labels are merged, the paper multiplies the scores:

s(z)=∏t=1Tqt,log⁡s(z)=∑t=1Tlog⁡qt.s(z)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q_t,\qquad \log s(z)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q_t.s(z)=t=1∏T​qt​,logs(z)=t=1∑T​logqt​.

At 0.98 per step, five steps give about 0.904 and twenty steps about 0.668, so the length enters the ranking through the aggregation rule. Reading the product as “the probability that the whole solution is correct” works only if the step scores are well calibrated, and ordinary classifier outputs usually are not calibrated without extra care.

Taking the minimum step score instead emphasizes the weakest point, but it hides the difference between one risky step and several moderate risks that add up. In Appendix F of the paper, merging positive and neutral gives 78.2% with the product and 77.6% with the minimum, while treating neutral as negative gives 77.4% and 77.8%. So building a PRM involves several choices beyond the step labels themselves: the meaning of the labels, the segmentation of the steps and the aggregation of the scores all shape which candidate is chosen.

The training data shapes the comparison as well. The paper deliberately sent annotators samples that the evaluator preferred but whose final answers were wrong, specifically to train the recognition of convincing errors. The large-scale PRM and ORM differ in the construction and the amount of their data, and separate small-scale controlled comparisons exist, so each experiment must be read under its own conditions before the gain can be explained.

Stage 3: training on your own successes (STaR)

If the model is sometimes right, the successful trajectories can be used to update it, instead of sampling widely every time. STaR turns this idea into iterated data generation plus supervised fine-tuning (paper).

The training data contains only the question xxx and the reference answer yyy, without human reasoning. The model generates reasoning and an answer, and only the correct results are kept. Questions that fail can go through rationalization: the correct answer is put into the prompt, the model tries to produce reasoning that reaches it, the result is filtered, and it is used as a training target for fine-tuning with the answer hint removed.

This step tells the model where the finish line is. Once the model knows the destination, valid backward derivations appear, but so do explanations that are put together only to reach the given answer. Checking only the final answer still cannot tell Student A from Student B.

Outcome rewards show both why this kind of filtering works and why it is not enough. The idealized objective is

J(θ)=E(z,a)∼πθ(⋅∣x)[1{a=y}],J(\theta)= \mathbb E_{(z,a)\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)} [\mathbf1\{a=y\}],J(θ)=E(z,a)∼πθ​(⋅∣x)​[1{a=y}],

with the policy gradient

∇θJ=E[1{a=y}∇θlog⁡πθ(z,a∣x)].\nabla_\theta J =\mathbb E[ \mathbf1\{a=y\}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(z,a\mid x)].∇θ​J=E[1{a=y}∇θ​logπθ​(z,a∣x)].

Correct trajectories receive a signal to raise their own probability, but the reward does not say which step deserved it. So valid derivations, filler and invalid steps that happened to reach the right answer are all reinforced together, and Student B receives the same reward as Student A.

The real STaR also involves greedy generation, filtering, answer hints and multi-step SFT, and it fine-tunes from the initial model again in each round, so it is not an unbiased on-policy estimate of this gradient. The formula explains why successes provide a direction, while the direction that training actually takes depends on how the data is generated and filtered.

With VVV meaning that the reasoning is valid and AAA meaning that the final answer is correct, the share of valid reasoning after filtering is

P(V∣A)=P(A∣V)P(V)P(A∣V)P(V)+P(A∣¬V)P(¬V).P(V\mid A)= \frac{P(A\mid V)P(V)} {P(A\mid V)P(V)+P(A\mid\neg V)P(\neg V)}.P(V∣A)=P(A∣V)P(V)+P(A∣¬V)P(¬V)P(A∣V)P(V)​.

As long as invalid reasoning can also reach the right answer, the filtered data is not clean process supervision. This problem is especially easy to overlook when the answer space is small, when the options contain hints, or when the grader checks only one number. Conversely, valuable reasoning that fails at the last step is thrown away completely, so the data drifts toward problems that the original model already found easy.

The results of the paper with GPT-J 6B show exactly this effect of the starting success rate:

CommonsenseQA is a multiple-choice task with five options and a random baseline of 20%, while GSM8K starts much lower, so far fewer trajectories survive the filtering. The table shows the best results near the saturation of the iterations, and the training subsets and iteration budgets of the methods are not fully aligned, so it is not a comparison with equal budgets.

The contribution of STaR is that a few successful trajectories can improve the distribution of generations. The problem that it leaves is just as direct: how can problems on which the model almost never succeeds enter the training? Answer hints help, but at the cost of passing the question “was the reasoning valid?” back to verification.

Stage 4: outcome-reward RL, sharpening vs. coverage

Moving from filtered SFT to training with outcome rewards does not remove the coverage problem. DeepSeek-R1 shows what large-scale training with verifiable rewards can achieve. In the first version of the paper, R1-Zero and the full R1 have different sources of supervision: R1-Zero skips the initial SFT but still relies on pretraining plus answer and format rewards, while the full R1 adds a cold start, RL, rejection sampling and SFT (R1 v1).

If the accuracy of the first answer rises after training, there are two possible reasons. Problems that the model occasionally solved may have become reliable, or problems that it almost never solved may have become solvable. Both changes are useful, and different metrics are needed to see them.

A constructed example shows that the two can move in opposite directions. Split the problems into two equal groups. Before training, the single-sample success is (0.9,0.01)(0.9,0.01)(0.9,0.01), and after training it is (0.99,0)(0.99,0)(0.99,0). The mean accuracy of the first answer rises from 45.5% to 49.5%. With 256 independent samples allowed, however, the coverage goes from about 96.2% before training to only about 50% after training. So the training raised the success on the easy group and erased the rare successes on the other group. (These values are given probabilities, not a trained model, but they are enough to show two metrics that move in opposite directions.)

Figure 3: Left, coverage before and after the constructed “training.” Right, the probability that a sampled group contains both successes and failures.

Yue et al. show such a separation in a real configuration. Qwen2.5-7B was trained on 2000 Omni-MATH-Rule problems, with 821 problems for the in-domain test and MATH500 for the out-of-domain test. In Table 4, the in-domain pass@1 rises from 10.2% to 28.3% after 450 GRPO steps, while pass@256 drops from 69.1% to 63.9%. On the other hand, the MATH500 pass@256 after 150 steps is 97.2%, above the 96.2% of the base model. The effect varies with the task and the training stage, so it does not follow that RL always reduces coverage (paper v4).

“Wrong all 256 times” also does not mean that the distribution contains no correct solution. For a fixed problem with independent samples and zero successes, the one-sided 95% upper bound is

pupper=1−0.051/256≈1.163%.p_{\mathrm{upper}}=1-0.05^{1/256}\approx1.163\%.pupper​=1−0.051/256≈1.163%.

If the true success rate is only 0.1%, the probability of 256 failures in a row is still about 77.4%. So a finite budget measures the observable coverage, a softer quantity than a hard limit on what the model can do.

Grading rules also change the coverage. Wen et al. require both a correct answer and reasoning that passes a check, and they compare CoT-Pass@K for Qwen2.5-32B and DAPO-Qwen-32B, with DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B as the evaluator of the reasoning and any/all/majority rules over repeated judgments (paper v2). This adds a view beyond the final answers, with the trade-off that the evaluator of the reasoning can make its own mistakes.

When every sample in a group is wrong

Common group-relative rewards sample GGG solutions per problem and build the following advantage from binary rewards:

Ai=ri−rˉσr+ε.A_i=\frac{r_i-\bar r}{\sigma_r+\varepsilon}.Ai​=σr​+εri​−rˉ​.

If the whole group is wrong, or the whole group is right, the numerator is zero, so this advantage term has nothing to compare within the group. (Other problems, KL or auxiliary losses can still produce gradients, so the whole training step does not do nothing.)

Under independent sampling with a single-sample success ppp, the probability that a group contains both successes and failures is

Pmixed=1−(1−p)G−pG.P_{\mathrm{mixed}}=1-(1-p)^G-p^G.Pmixed​=1−(1−p)G−pG.

This is the right panel of Figure 3. Very hard problems are usually all wrong and very easy problems usually all right, so only the middle band reliably provides a contrast between outcomes. In addition, this probability only shows whether a signal exists, not whether the reward is attributed to valid reasoning.

The curriculum RL of Cai et al. builds on this observation with a loop of probing, bridging with teacher SFT, and curriculum GRPO. It estimates the current difficulty and then uses a few teacher trajectories to move the model into a region in which it produces successes (preprint v1). Here the teacher really contributes methods, so the gain comes from the teacher and the curriculum together. A comparison of teacher SFT, uniform RL and curriculum RL on the same problems and the same budget of teacher tokens would show how much each part adds.

This is the same starting point as the answer hints of STaR. Filtering or relative rewards alone can hardly extract a success signal from trajectories that always fail. External information changes this, but it must be counted as part of the training process.

Stage 5: making “check again” a training task (SCoRe)

All candidates so far were generated independently. Revision is different: round two sees round one, so the new trajectory is conditioned on the old text. Sometimes the model finds the mistake, sometimes it stays anchored to the original answer, and sometimes it breaks an answer that was right.

The arithmetic of revision

Let the accuracy of the first round be aaa, the fraction of the original errors that are fixed be uuu, and the fraction of the original correct answers that are broken be vvv. The accuracy of the second round is

a2=a(1−v)+(1−a)u,a_2=a(1-v)+(1-a)u,a2​=a(1−v)+(1−a)u,

with the net change

a2−a=(1−a)u−av.a_2-a=(1-a)u-av.a2​−a=(1−a)u−av.

If only “how many errors were fixed” is reported, the second term disappears. The stronger the first round is, the fewer errors remain to be fixed, and the more correct answers can be broken.

Figure 4: The four transitions: right→right, right→wrong, wrong→right, wrong→wrong.

Construct 100 paired records: 49 right→right, 21 right→wrong, 18 wrong→right and 12 wrong→wrong. The repair rate is 18/30=60%18/30=60\%18/30=60% and sounds very good, yet the final accuracy drops from 70% to 67%.

Figure 5: The constructed 100-record example.

An oracle that knows the reference and keeps the correct answer of the two rounds would get 88 right, while simply submitting round two gets 67. The gap of 21 questions consists exactly of the broken first answers. Capturing this gain requires the system to learn when to keep and when to change an answer, and a selection after the fact cannot be credited to the generator.

Why “teach it to fix wrong answers” isn’t enough

It seems possible to collect the wrong first answers of an old model, pair them with correct revisions and train on these pairs. At test time, however, the first answer comes from the current trained model, and this model has a different error distribution. Offline training conditions on

y1∼πold(⋅∣x),y2∼πθ(⋅∣x,y1,pcheck),y_1\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(\cdot\mid x), \qquad y_2\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y_1,p_{\mathrm{check}}),y1​∼πold​(⋅∣x),y2​∼πθ​(⋅∣x,y1​,pcheck​),

while at test time both rounds come from the current policy. So fixing given first answers well does not guarantee fixing the own first answers of the current model well, and Figure 5 of SCoRe compares exactly these two conditions.

Another shortcut is to copy the first answer as closely as possible. This never breaks anything, but it also almost never fixes anything. Some Pair-SFT configurations with right-to-right trajectories in the paper show zero changes in both directions and identical accuracy in both rounds. So raising only the accuracy of round two does not necessarily reward useful revision.

The first stage of SCoRe raises the reward of round two while it puts a strong KL constraint on round one. This constraint keeps round one near the base model, so that training does not remove the states that “need fixing” too early. (Round two keeps its usual KL regularization as well.)

The second stage optimizes both rounds jointly, with a progress reward. With the binary outcome rewards r1,r2r_1,r_2r1​,r2​:

r2′=r2+α(r2−r1).r'_2=r_2+\alpha(r_2-r_1).r2′​=r2​+α(r2​−r1​).

With α=10\alpha=10α=10, as in the experiments:

Fixing an answer earns a large reward, and breaking a correct answer receives a heavy penalty. However, it matters which round this reward updates. Treating r1+r2′=(1−α)r1+(1+α)r2r_1+r'_2=(1-\alpha)r_1+(1+\alpha)r_2r1​+r2′​=(1−α)r1​+(1+α)r2​ as the return of round one would give the strange result of a negative coefficient on a correct first answer. Appendix A.2 of the paper uses immediate rewards for each round (effectively γ=0\gamma=0γ=0), so the progress term of round two does not enter the policy gradient of round one as a future return.

Immediate rewards do not make the rounds independent, though, because the parameters are shared, and updates for round two still change future first answers. For this reason, the constraints of the stages and the ablations matter. In Table 4, a model without multi-turn training reaches 61.8% on the first answer and then drops to 59.4% after checking. Removing stage one gives 59.2%→61.4%, removing the progress reward gives 60.0%→62.6%, and the full method gives 60.0%→64.4%. So a configuration with a stronger first answer did not automatically become a better reviser.

During training, the rewards come from reference answers or program tests. At test time, round two has only its own response and a generic instruction to check, without any hint of whether round one was right, and this setting is what “intrinsic self-correction” refers to. The code experiments use a different model, Gemini 1.0 Pro, trained on MBPP and evaluated on HumanEval, so they are best read separately from the math table. In addition, more rounds do not keep helping forever, because a policy trained for two rounds meets unfamiliar situations if it keeps revising.

Is revision worth the budget?

Learning to revise is not the final step. Round two costs new generation and a longer context, and the alternative is not only to stop at this point: the system could also generate another independent sample and use the same selector.

Section 6.2 of SCoRe compares generating 2K2K2K samples in parallel with generating KKK samples and revising each one once, both with self-consistency. In that setting, spending part of the candidate budget on revision is worth it. However, equal candidate counts do not mean equal tokens or FLOPs, and the input cost of round two is larger (§6.2).

To test this question on a specific model, keep the first answers on a batch of problems that were not used in training, and run three branches: submit the first answer directly, resample with the same total token budget, and revise while reading the first answer. The selector and the grader should be fixed in advance, and the input and output tokens should be logged separately. If revision beats the first answer but loses to resampling with the same budget, the extra compute is genuinely useful, but the self-correction structure has not yet shown an advantage.

The unit of comparison should be the problem. Generating many times for one problem does not turn it into many independent problems, so keeping the pairing and aggregating by problem shows which problems a strategy actually changed. The cases of disagreement are the most useful: a right first answer broken by revision, a right answer with wrong reasoning, or a high evaluator score on a wrong choice. These cases show directly whether generation, selection or revision should be fixed next.

Conclusion

From CoT to SCoRe, each method moved a different position into training. CoT made intermediate text part of the generation. Sampling and selection separated coverage from choice, and PRMs judged candidates at the level of steps. STaR and outcome-reward RL fed successes back into the weights, sharpening what the model could already do and sometimes giving up rare successes. SCoRe trained the model to face its own first answer. So the accuracy of a single stage does not explain the other stages.

The solution of Student B, with two wrong steps and a right answer, shows the differences when it passes through each system. CoT allows it to be written, and self-consistency may vote for it. An ORM rewards it, while a PRM may catch the error at step one. STaR trains on it, RL reinforces it together with Student A, and SCoRe asks whether the model would notice the error on a second reading. Each system gives a different answer.

A correct final answer is one bit of evidence; every method in this post is a different way of deciding what this bit may teach.

Environment and measurement method

No language model was trained or sampled locally; every model performance number is the original paper’s result at the linked version, checked against its appendices. Locally, only numerical scripts were run: NumPy generates constructed data for paired transitions, binomial sampling, step products and group rewards from the formulas, and recomputes the net gains in SCoRe Tables 2 and 4.

python bench_mur/systems/reasoning_metric_evidence.py

Materials are in rep_mur/blog-rewrite/systems/reasoning/, including inputs, CSVs, transcribed paper tables and versions.

Reproduction advice and source limits

If you run your own model experiments, save problem IDs and splits, model and prompt, temperature and length limits, full text of every round, visible feedback, input and output tokens, the grader and the final choice. Paired tables must keep both rounds, not just summary accuracy, or you can’t recover where fixes and breaks came from.

The local PDFs of the original CoT and STaR papers don’t include all external appendices, so the available material isn’t enough to rebuild full training recipes. The PRM and SCoRe methods, tables and appendices used here were read and checked at the versions linked in the text.
