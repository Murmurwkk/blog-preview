Out-of-Range L2 Hit Rates in Nsight Compute: Raw Counters and Profiling Passes

Two attention profiles reported L2 hit rates above 100%. I followed the discrepancy from the raw reports to the hit and total counters, then checked how the GB206 backend schedules their collection.

Nsight Compute said the L2 cache on my GPU had hit on about 102% of lookups during a softmax kernel. A second attention kernel came in at about 101%. Both programs returned correct outputs, and the profiler exited normally. I wanted to know where the extra hits entered the calculation. The captures came from the INT8 KV-cache experiment and the online-softmax experiment.

I kept the original reports, collected two diagnostic batches of 60 reports each, and queried the counter backend shipped with the same Nsight Compute release. Two observations narrowed the search.

  • In captures that saved the underlying counts and showed the anomaly, the raw hit count exceeds the raw total count. The reported percentage agrees with 100 × hits / total.
  • For this GPU, the backend places the hit count and the total count in separate isolated passes, even when both counts are requested together.

Which run-to-run activity makes the two counts disagree is still an open question.

Out-of-range L2 hit rates in the original NCU reports

I read the original .ncu-rep files through NVIDIA’s Python Report Interface, before my export code or plotting script had touched them. The two percentages were already above 100%. The CSV export rounded their last digits.

Both reports used application replay. In application replay, Nsight Compute runs the whole application once for each pass and collects a different group of counters in each pass. The original reports captured these two launches.

These durations describe the profiled captures and do not replace uninstrumented timing. One kernel is very short, whereas the other runs far longer than the often-mentioned steady-state scale of about 20 µs, so a short kernel cannot explain both samples.

The source code suggested one more possibility. The softmax writes its result in place, so a replay could in principle apply softmax a second time to the probabilities of the previous pass. The KV entry point, however, recomputes the scores with QK before every softmax and PV, and the online entry point reads fixed Q, K and V. I found no call path in which the input changes from one replay to the next.

L2 hit sectors exceed total lookup sectors

An NVIDIA engineer’s definition of the global metric gave me a numerator and a denominator to look up.

r=100lts__t_sectors_lookup_hit.avglts__t_sectors.avg.r=100\frac{\texttt{lts\_\_t\_sectors\_lookup\_hit.avg}} {\texttt{lts\_\_t\_sectors.avg}}.r=100lts__t_sectors.avglts__t_sectors_lookup_hit.avg​.

L2 tracks memory in 32-byte sectors. The ratio counts every global L2 sector lookup, not only the requests that come from the L1TEX units of the SMs. When the profiler collects the numerator and the denominator in different passes, the two counts can come from different executions. Activity from other engines on the GPU can also change counters on shared resources (NVIDIA’s explanation).

Because the original reports did not save these two component counts, the three passes alone cannot show which execution supplied the numerator and which execution supplied the denominator. The first new batch, Stage 1, kept the old binary, the inputs, the 20 warm-ups and the complete entry point. Stage 1 changed the metric requests by adding the global hit, miss and total counts as .sum and .avg, plus the same counts restricted to L1TEX requests.

In the table below, H, M and T are the global hit, miss and total sector counts, all taken as .sum. No read-only sector count or DRAM byte count is used as the denominator.

The native hit rate agrees with 100H/T to the rounding of the export, and the .avg counts give the same ratio. In the first row there are 41,022 more hits than total lookups. A single population of lookups cannot have that property. This locates the discrepancy in the two component counts I received.

A bounded formula would hide this inconsistency. With 100H/(H+M), the first row gives almost 100% and the second gives exactly 100%. For non-negative H and M, this formula always stays between 0 and 100%, because the formula never compares H with the separately collected T. Although restricting the counts to L1TEX requests helps to show where the traffic comes from, a value between 0 and 100% still does not certify that a capture is accurate.

GB206 counter scheduling and replay passes on the GPU

Stage 1 crossed six metric configurations with the two original cases and ran five independent process starts per cell, for 60 reports in total. Each round shuffled the order of all 12 combinations. The KV entry point captures three kernels and the online entry point captures one, so Stage 1 produced 120 kernel records. The five starts per cell are descriptive repeats for locating the problem, so the five starts do not estimate how often the anomaly occurs, and the profiled durations are not used as performance samples.

The clearest counterexample comes from the online case. When only the percentage was requested, four of five captures exceeded 100%. One capture with extra suffixes turned off reached about 121%. That configuration omitted H, M and T, leaving its component counts unavailable. In these data, requesting fewer metrics neither made the collection single-pass nor removed the anomaly. When all five captures of a cell stay in range, the result only shows that those five captures stayed in range.

A derived metric can need several passes because it depends on several raw counters. To test this point for this GPU, I used the nvperf_host.dll that ships with Nsight Compute 2026.2.1 and queried the virtual chip gb206 through the PerfWorks host API. Each query added all raw dependencies of one request to a single pass group, asked for isolated collection and let the backend schedule the passes.

I submitted H and T together in one group. The backend itself split their dependencies across two passes. This was an offline scheduling query for virtual chip GB206 using the same tool version. It ran without a CUDA driver, device context or counter-availability image from the physical GPU. A mapping from each counter to the actual pass in a captured report would be stronger evidence.

The two passes from the backend and the three passes of the real capture belong to different levels, and the current report interface does not say which pass collected each dependency. Without more evidence, the extra pass should not be read as a warm-up or as a state restoration, and the percentage should not be divided by three. The reports list zero profiler warm-up passes.

Out-of-range readings persist with 512 thread blocks

Another explanation was that eight CTAs give the GPU too little work, so other activity can dominate the counts. A CTA is NVIDIA’s name for a thread block. Stage 2 moved the same softmax into a separate program, fixed N=4096N=4096N=4096 and swept HHH over 8, 36, 144 and 512, with one CTA per head.

Before profiling, I checked the identity and the correctness of the separate program. The softmax in the separate program and the softmax in the original binary have the same 504 SASS instructions, compared after removing addresses and encoding comments and normalizing whitespace. The main sweep and an extra boundary check at H=3H=3H=3, N=257N=257N=257 ran in six processes with seven outputs. All outputs passed the independent FP64 reference, the row-sum check, the input bit-pattern check and the boundary-guard check. During the formal capture, every replayed process also recorded a fingerprint of its GPU input and checked its output.

Stage 2 used three metric configurations, pct-only, global and tex, with five captures per cell, for 60 reports in total. All 220 application starts during replay passed the input, output and guard checks.

Out-of-range values appeared again at 144 blocks and at 512 blocks. At 512 blocks, the profiled kernels lasted about 46–65 µs. Increasing work beyond the commonly cited 20 µs duration still left samples above the physical limit.

Stage 2 also changes more than one factor. Changing HHH changes the amount of work, the data footprint and the duration together. The separate program restores the logits with a device-to-device copy and checks the input with a device-to-host copy, so its cache history differs from the original QK → softmax entry point. Because of these differences, the Stage 2 readings of about 70% to 80% cannot replace the readings near 100% from the original program. The gap between the two sets of percentages also does not show that the cache behavior of the original operator became worse.

Limits of repeated sampling and the unresolved pass-to-pass discrepancy

Repetition reduces random error, but repetition cannot repair a mismatch between the counts. For random errors that are unbiased and stable under fixed conditions, more independent repeats reduce the uncertainty of the mean. For drift between runs, the timing experiments on this blog use interleaved pairs, A/A calibration and confirmation in independent processes. When the numerator and the denominator do not describe the same lookups, more repeats do not repair the ratio. Averaging values above 100% down to 99% does not turn them back into the hit rate of one consistent set of lookups.

Here is where the evidence leaves me.

  • Checked directly: the anomaly is present in the native reports; the new reports contain H > T; the native hit rate equals 100H/T; the kernels pass their correctness checks; and the anomaly also appears in a separate program with the same softmax SASS.
  • Scheduling evidence: the GB206 backend of the same version places the dependencies of H and T in different isolated passes, and real pct-only captures still used three application replay passes. This evidence supports the explanation that the ratio combines counts from different executions, although a per-dependency pass mapping from a real report is still missing.

The earlier correctness checks and the paired timings without the profiler remain valid. The KV-cache and online-softmax posts keep the two original values as recorded and mark them invalid. What becomes invalid is any use of these percentages to estimate miss counts or DRAM traffic. For future cache analysis, I will first save the matching raw counts, their units, the actual number of replay passes and the spread across repeats, and only then decide whether these data can support a conclusion. An in-range percentage still needs that check. NVIDIA Profiling Guide, range and precision

Which activity changed the counts between passes? Other GPU engines, the cache state, the collection boundary and the tool itself are still possible contributors. These captures do not identify one of them. I would particularly like to see the per-pass counter mapping of a real report.

Experimental setup, exact capture details and private evidence

At 512 blocks the measured range of profiled durations was 45.760–65.344 µs. The alternative bounded formula 100H/(H+M) gives 99.999017% for Stage 1 job 19. The original decimal percentages are in the first table.

The GPU was an RTX 5070 Laptop GPU with the GB206 chip and compute capability sm_120. The software was CUDA 13.1 and Nsight Compute 2026.2.1. All captures used application replay, --cache-control none, --clock-control none and 20 warm-ups. Both batches ran serially from plans that were frozen before collection, and no display, driver or power setting was changed. Although power and clock snapshots were saved before and after the batches, no continuous trace was recorded during the captures, so this post makes no claim about constant clocks or power. Counter collection was kept separate from the formal timing runs.

The source files, the complete commands, the compile and SASS checks, all 120 CSV exports with their per-run checks, the offline scheduling query and the plotting entry point are described in bench_mur/l2_hit_rate_investigation/README.md. The full .ncu-rep files stay in the local result archive, and their SHA-256 hashes are recorded. A small evidence package in the private project checkout keeps the original export bytes and a manifest.
