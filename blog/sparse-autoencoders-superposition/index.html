Testing Sparse Autoencoders on Synthetic Data with Known Features

I hid 12 known patterns in synthetic data and asked a sparse autoencoder to find them. With 4 active parts per input instead of 2, its error fell threefold, but it found only half of the patterns.

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are the main tool that interpretability researchers use to look inside language models. The idea is simple: take the vector of numbers that a model computes at some layer, and rewrite it as a sum of a few pieces chosen from a learned list, in the hope that each piece means one thing. SAEs are usually judged by how well this sum rebuilds the original vector.

I wanted to know whether rebuilding well means finding the right pieces, so I built a test with a known answer: 12 hidden patterns, mixed a few at a time into 8 numbers, and an SAE that must recover them. With a dictionary of 24 entries and at most 2 pieces per input, the SAE found all 12 patterns in every run. When it could use 4, its reconstruction error fell about threefold, from 0.049 to 0.016, but it found only half of the patterns.

Reconstruction got better, but discovery got worse, and this is not a training bug. An SAE is trained to rebuild its input from few pieces. Finding the pieces that the data was actually made of is what we want from the SAE, but it is not what the SAE is trained to do, and the same vector can often be built in more than one way.

The post has four parts. The first part explains why the patterns of a model are hard to separate at all, with an example that can be checked by hand. The second part describes what an SAE computes and the three ways (L1, TopK and JumpReLU) of limiting it to few pieces. The third part presents the experiment: 27 training runs, what they measured and what they show. The last part discusses what a real model needs in order to move from “reconstructs well” to “explains the model”.

Why the patterns are hard to find

Interpretability work calls a pattern a feature: something that is either absent or present with some strength, such as “this text contains a date”. Inside a model, a feature appears as a fixed direction in the activation vector, scaled by its strength. If a model had one dimension per feature, each feature could be read from its own coordinate, but models do not work this way.

Two features can share one number

Take two features with non-negative strengths f1f_1f1​ and f2f_2f2​, and store only their difference in one number:

h=f1−f2.h=f_1-f_2.h=f1​−f2​.

As long as the two features never appear together, both can be read back exactly:

f^1=ReLU⁡(h),f^2=ReLU⁡(−h).\hat f_1=\operatorname{ReLU}(h),\qquad \hat f_2=\operatorname{ReLU}(-h).f^​1​=ReLU(h),f^​2​=ReLU(−h).

The sign shows which feature is present, and the size shows how strong it is, so one number holds two features. The cost appears when both features are present at the same time. (f1,f2)=(0.8,0.3)(f_1,f_2)=(0.8,0.3)(f1​,f2​)=(0.8,0.3) and (0.5,0)(0.5,0)(0.5,0) both give h=0.5h=0.5h=0.5, so the reader can only answer (0.5,0)(0.5,0)(0.5,0), and the squared error is 2min⁡(f1,f2)22\min(f_1,f_2)^22min(f1​,f2​)2. However, if each feature is present independently with probability ppp, such a collision happens only with probability p2p^2p2. For sufficiently rare features, an occasional collision costs less than dropping one of the features entirely.

This is superposition: a model stores more features than it has dimensions by giving them overlapping, non-orthogonal directions, and it relies on the fact that these features rarely appear together. Toy Models of Superposition showed that small networks learn exactly this trade-off and pack more features into a space as the features become sparser.

In ddd dimensions with m>dm>dm>d features, the activation is a weighted sum of directions:

h=D∗f=∑j=1mdjfj,D∗∈Rd×m.h=D_*f=\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_jf_j,\qquad D_*\in\mathbb R^{d\times m}.h=D∗​f=j=1∑m​dj​fj​,D∗​∈Rd×m.

Each column djd_jdj​ of D∗D_*D∗​ is the direction of one feature, and fj≥0f_j\ge0fj​≥0 is its strength. A single coordinate then mixes many features, hi=∑j(D∗)ijfjh_i=\sum_j(D_*)_{ij}f_jhi​=∑j​(D∗​)ij​fj​, and for this reason, studying what makes one neuron fire often does not give that neuron a single meaning.

Even sparsity doesn’t make the answer unique

One might hope that “use as few features as possible” selects the right decomposition, but this is not always true. Place five unit directions evenly around a circle, 72° apart. Then two of them add up to a shorter copy of the direction between them:

d0+d2=2cos⁡(72∘) d1≈0.618 d1.d_0+d_2=2\cos(72^\circ)\,d_1\approx0.618\,d_1.d0​+d2​=2cos(72∘)d1​≈0.618d1​.

Figure 1: Five directions in two dimensions. The marked point can be built from two directions, d0 + d2, or from one, 0.618·d1. NumPy confirms the identity to within 10⁻¹⁴. The directions are geometric, with no meaning attached.

If the data was really generated by d0+d2d_0+d_2d0​+d2​, a method that prefers fewer active pieces will choose the wrong explanation, 0.618 d10.618\,d_10.618d1​. Telling the two explanations apart requires statistics over many samples, such as which directions often appear alone. If the data does not contain this distinction, no reconstruction objective can supply it.

What an SAE computes

In a real model, we can collect as many activation vectors hhh as we like, but we never see the true directions D∗D_*D∗​ or strengths fff. An SAE tries to learn both. Its encoder estimates strengths from hhh, and its decoder rebuilds hhh from them:

u=Wenc(h−b)+benc,z=ReLU⁡(u),h^=Dz+b.u=W_{\mathrm{enc}}(h-b)+b_{\mathrm{enc}},\qquad z=\operatorname{ReLU}(u),\qquad \hat h=Dz+b.u=Wenc​(h−b)+benc​,z=ReLU(u),h^=Dz+b.

The entries of zzz are called latents, and the columns of the decoder form the dictionary of the SAE, with one learned direction per latent. The width mmm of the dictionary is usually larger than ddd.

The encoder and the decoder do different jobs. A decoder column says “when this latent is on, the activation moves in this direction”, while an encoder row says “this is the input that turns the latent on”. The two are not simply transposes of each other: even with the perfect dictionary D=D∗D=D_*D=D∗​, the detector D⊤h=D⊤D∗fD^\top h=D^\top D_*fD⊤h=D⊤D∗​f also responds to other features, because non-orthogonal directions overlap. Training often starts the encoder as the transpose of the decoder, but keeping the two tied is an extra assumption.

The simplest SAE minimizes

L=Eh[12∥h−h^∥22+λ∥z∥1].\mathcal L= \mathbb E_h\left[ \tfrac12\lVert h-\hat h\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert z\rVert_1 \right].L=Eh​[21​∥h−h^∥22​+λ∥z∥1​].

The first term asks for an accurate rebuild, and the second term charges for the total size of the latents. Without the second term, a sufficiently wide autoencoder can simply copy its input, so the penalty makes the way the SAE rebuilds the input part of the goal.

This penalty has a loophole. If a decoder column is scaled up by ccc and its latent is scaled down by ccc, the rebuild stays the same while the penalty shrinks. Implementations therefore fix the length of the decoder columns or charge ∑j∣zj∣ ∥dj∥2\sum_j\lvert z_j\rvert\,\lVert d_j\rVert_2∑j​∣zj​∣∥dj​∥2​ instead. The experiment below keeps every column at unit length. This determines the scale, but not the order of the latents, duplicate directions or the ambiguity from Figure 1.

The width of the dictionary and the number of latents allowed at the same time are separate choices. Towards Monosemanticity analyzed an SAE with 4,096 latents on a 512-dimensional MLP layer: it offered eight times more candidate directions, while each input still turned on only a few latents. If both choices change together and the error drops, it is impossible to tell whether more options or more active latents per input caused the drop.

Three ways to keep it sparse

L1 shrinks every active latent

To see what the L1 penalty does, consider one latent alone. Let the projection of the input onto its direction be t>0t>0t>0 and the value of the latent be a≥0a\ge0a≥0. The best aaa has a closed form:

min⁡a≥012(t−a)2+λ1a⟹a∗=max⁡(t−λ1,0).\min_{a\ge0}\tfrac12(t-a)^2+\lambda_1a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^*=\max(t-\lambda_1,0).a≥0min​21​(t−a)2+λ1​a⟹a∗=max(t−λ1​,0).

So L1 does not only switch small latents off; it also subtracts λ1\lambda_1λ1​ from every latent that stays on. Because the penalty is paid per unit of size, it shrinks everything.

L0 only charges for switching on

If instead each active latent costs a fixed λ0\lambda_0λ0​ (an L0 penalty), the latent is either off and costs t2/2t^2/2t2/2 in error, or on at full size ttt and costs λ0\lambda_0λ0​:

min⁡a≥012(t−a)2+λ01[a>0]⟹a∗={t,t>2λ0,0,t<2λ0.\min_{a\ge0}\tfrac12(t-a)^2+\lambda_0\mathbf1[a>0] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a^*= \begin{cases} t,&t>\sqrt{2\lambda_0},\\ 0,&t<\sqrt{2\lambda_0}. \end{cases}a≥0min​21​(t−a)2+λ0​1[a>0]⟹a∗={t,0,​t>2λ0​​,t<2λ0​​.​

This scalar case does not solve a whole non-orthogonal dictionary, but it shows the difference: it is possible to limit how many latents are on without shrinking the latents that are on.

TopK: a fixed budget per input

TopK keeps only the kkk largest pre-activations of each input. Gao et al. trained SAEs in this way at a large scale, and the core of their public implementation is a few lines:

topk = torch.topk(x, k=self.k, dim=-1)
values = self.postact_fn(topk.values)
result = torch.zeros_like(x)
result.scatter_(-1, topk.indices, values)
return result

Here x holds the pre-activations of the encoder for one input, so the latents compete within each input. The ReLU (postact_fn) runs after the selection, so an input whose pre-activations are all negative, for example [−5,−3,−1,−7][-5,-3,-1,-7][−5,−3,−1,−7] with k=2k=2k=2, receives no active latents at all. TopK therefore allows at most kkk latents. The kept values are not shrunk in the way that L1 shrinks them, but they are still estimates of the encoder, not true strengths.

Because the cut-off is “the kkk largest”, it moves with each input, and a latent can drop out simply because other latents in the same input are stronger. This behavior suits a hard compute budget, but it ignores how complex the input is, so a simple input and a complex input receive the same number of slots.

JumpReLU: a learned threshold per latent

JumpReLU gives each latent its own threshold θj\theta_jθj​ and keeps the full value above it:

zj=uj 1[uj>θj],L=∥h−h^∥22+λ∥z∥0.z_j=u_j\,\mathbf1[u_j>\theta_j],\qquad \mathcal L=\lVert h-\hat h\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert z\rVert_0.zj​=uj​1[uj​>θj​],L=∥h−h^∥22​+λ∥z∥0​.

Different inputs can now turn on different numbers of latents. The difficulty is training the thresholds. For a fixed input, a small change in θj\theta_jθj​ changes nothing unless it crosses uju_juj​, so the ordinary gradient with respect to θj\theta_jθj​ is zero almost everywhere.

The average loss over many inputs, however, changes smoothly. Take the scalar example again, with inputs ttt spread evenly over [0,2][0,2][0,2] and the output a=t 1[t>θ]a=t\,\mathbf1[t>\theta]a=t1[t>θ]. Inputs below the threshold are switched off and cost their squared error, while inputs above the threshold cost λ0\lambda_0λ0​ each:

EL(θ)=∫0θt22⋅12 dt⏟switched off+λ0(1−θ2)⏟switched on=θ312+λ0(1−θ2),\mathbb E\mathcal L(\theta) =\underbrace{\int_0^\theta\frac{t^2}{2}\cdot\frac12\,dt}_{\text{switched off}} +\underbrace{\lambda_0\left(1-\frac\theta2\right)}_{\text{switched on}} =\frac{\theta^3}{12}+\lambda_0\left(1-\frac\theta2\right),EL(θ)=switched off∫0θ​2t2​⋅21​dt​​+switched onλ0​(1−2θ​)​​=12θ3​+λ0​(1−2θ​), d ELdθ=θ24−λ02.\frac{d\,\mathbb E\mathcal L}{d\theta}=\frac{\theta^2}{4}-\frac{\lambda_0}{2}.dθdEL​=4θ2​−2λ0​​.

The minimum is at θ=2λ0\theta=\sqrt{2\lambda_0}θ=2λ0​​, again 0.5 in Figure 2. Moving the threshold switches the inputs that lie directly next to it, and this small slice of probability is the source of the gradient. The JumpReLU paper estimates this gradient during training with a kernel KKK of width ε\varepsilonε around the threshold:

∂~ JumpReLU⁡θ(u)∂θ=−θε K ⁣(u−θε).\frac{\widetilde\partial\,\operatorname{JumpReLU}_\theta(u)}{\partial\theta} =-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\, K\!\left(\frac{u-\theta}{\varepsilon}\right).∂θ∂JumpReLUθ​(u)​=−εθ​K(εu−θ​).

A wider ε\varepsilonε uses more inputs but reaches further from the threshold, while a narrower ε\varepsilonε is more local but noisier. The scalar example only shows why such an estimate is needed, and the experiment below uses TopK.

The data does not come from a language model; it is built so that the right answer is known.

What it shows

The most important result is in the last two rows. At width 24 with budget 2, all three runs found all 12 hidden directions. With budget 4, the reconstruction error fell from 0.049 to 0.016, but the runs found only between 17% and 58% of the directions. If the SAE were chosen by reconstruction error, the choice would be the SAE that found fewer of the real directions.

From the point of view of the objective, this result is not surprising. The SAE is asked to rebuild hhh accurately, not to use D∗D_*D∗​. With four latents per input, it can spread one hidden direction over several imperfectly aligned latents and use the spare latents to correct the remaining error, so the rebuild improves while the link between latents and hidden directions becomes unclear. The table shows this mismatch: the inputs contain about one hidden direction on average, yet the SAEs with k=4k=4k=4 use all four latents on almost every input.

This result is not a general verdict against k=4k=4k=4. If inputs often contained four or five directions at once, a small budget would force the SAE to lose information, and with more similar hidden directions, noisy data or a different training budget, the best setting could change. What the experiment supports is narrower: on data with a known answer, a lower reconstruction error did not mean that the SAE found the true directions.

A wider dictionary also has a cost. In the three runs with width 24 and budget 2, 8%, 17% and 21% of the latents (2, 4 and 5 of 24) never switched on anywhere in the test set. This count comes from a finite test set and cannot be compared directly with the “dead latent” rates that papers report over long training runs, but it shows that reconstruction, sparsity and the use of the dictionary should be read together.

From reconstruction to behavior

A real model has no answer key. The first property that can be checked is whether the model still works when its activation hhh is replaced by the rebuild h^\hat hh^ of the SAE.

The activation error alone cannot answer this question, because not all errors matter equally. If the next layer reads only 100h1100h_1100h1​, an error of 0.01 in h1h_1h1​ changes its output by 1, while the same error in h2h_2h2​ changes nothing. For this reason, SAE papers insert the reconstruction into the model and measure how much the next-token loss increases, instead of reporting only the reconstruction error.

The insertion usually goes through an activation hook. In TransformerLens, a HookPoint is just an identity layer at a named site (pinned source, MIT license):

def forward(self, x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
    return x

A function attached to this hook can record hhh for training, or it can return h^\hat hh^, so that the rest of the model sees the reconstruction. The site matters: an MLP output, an attention output and the residual stream are different things even when they have the same size, and a dictionary trained at one site does not transfer to another.

Only after the whole reconstruction is shown to be accurate does it make sense to study single latents, and even then there is a trap. Write the activation as the SAE sees it, with ϵ\epsilonϵ as the part that the SAE fails to reconstruct:

h=Dz+b+ϵ.h=Dz+b+\epsilon.h=Dz+b+ϵ.

Removing latent jjj alone should give

h′=h−djzj=b+∑i≠jdizi+ϵ.h'=h-d_jz_j=b+\sum_{i\ne j}d_iz_i+\epsilon.h′=h−dj​zj​=b+i=j∑​di​zi​+ϵ.

The residual ϵ\epsilonϵ stays. If b+∑i≠jdizib+\sum_{i\ne j}d_iz_ib+∑i=j​di​zi​ is fed in instead, both the latent and the residual are removed at once, and the measured effect no longer belongs to the latent alone.

Naming a latent needs the same care. If the inputs that activate a latent most strongly all share a topic, it is tempting to name the latent after that topic. These inputs, however, have now been used to choose the name, so the test must use other inputs: it should look for inputs that activate the latent without fitting the name, and for inputs that fit the name without activating the latent. The next step is to change the value of the latent and check whether the output of the model moves as the name predicts. Interventions should stay moderate, because pushing a latent far beyond its normal range takes the model into a region it never visits, and a change there says little about its normal computation.

Conclusion

Superposition lets a model store more features than it has dimensions, but it makes the decomposition ambiguous, and asking for sparsity does not remove this ambiguity. An SAE learns a sparse decomposition that rebuilds its input well, and L1, TopK and JumpReLU differ only in how they charge for sparsity. On data where the right answer was known, the decomposition that rebuilt the input best was not the right one. In a real model, closing this gap requires tests on held-out inputs and interventions in the computation of the model itself.

Reconstruction shows that the dictionary can rebuild the activation; only intervention shows that the model uses it.
