ParScale: Scaling a Language Model with Parallel Streams

This note explains how ParScale scales a model with parallel streams and refits its scaling law to the released data. With it, a reader can estimate what P streams are worth in parameters and what they cost.

ParScale is a method from the Qwen team’s paper Parallel Scaling Law for Language Models. It grows a language model by adding parallel computation instead of parameters. The model processes each input PPP times in parallel, uses a different learned prefix in each run, and combines the PPP outputs into one output. The paper claims that this third way to scale, next to parameters and training data, follows a scaling law of its own, and the team released 48 checkpoints, so readers can check the claim.

This note explains how ParScale works, what the streams inside a released checkpoint do, and how the paper’s scaling law converts streams into an equivalent number of parameters. It then compares the two forms of the law, compares ParScale with other ways to scale, analyzes the paper’s comparison with a 4.4B model, and measures the cost at inference. Two parts are my own verification: I refitted the scaling law to the paper’s loss tables, and I ran and timed the released 0.7B checkpoints on an RTX 5070 Laptop GPU. All other numbers come from the paper, its code and its checkpoints, listed at the end.

The analysis reaches three conclusions. First, on the one text that I probed, the 8 streams behave like a small ensemble whose members share all weights. Second, on code, 8 streams are worth about 1.85 times the parameters in my refit; this gain is large, but it is smaller than the 2.77 times of the 4.4B model that the paper’s headline compares against. Third, the current data cannot yet show whether the gain keeps growing with more streams or stops at some point.

How ParScale works

ParScale uses one set of weights and runs each input through these weights PPP times. Each run is called a stream. The streams differ only in a learned prefix: each stream has 48 learned tokens per layer, these tokens are stored in the stream’s KV cache, and as a result the same weights see a slightly different context in each stream.

After the last layer, a small two-layer MLP reads the PPP final hidden states and outputs one weight for each stream. The model then sends the weighted sum of the PPP outputs to the LM head:

g(x)=∑i=1Pwi f(xi),w=softmax(MLP([h1;… ;hP])).g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{P} w_i\, f(x_i), \qquad w=\mathrm{softmax}\big(\mathrm{MLP}([h_1;\dots;h_P])\big).g(x)=i=1∑P​wi​f(xi​),w=softmax(MLP([h1​;…;hP​])).

During training, the weights are smoothed toward a uniform distribution, wi←(1−ϵ)wi+ϵ/Pw_i \leftarrow (1-\epsilon)w_i+\epsilon/Pwi​←(1−ϵ)wi​+ϵ/P with ϵ=0.1\epsilon=0.1ϵ=0.1, so the model cannot turn off any stream completely. The method adds very few parameters. For the 1.6B model with P=8P=8P=8, the prefixes and the MLP add 24.2M parameters, about 1.5% of the total.

In the released code, the streams are implemented with batching. The code repeats the input PPP times along the batch dimension, places each copy’s own prefix in front of its KV cache, and processes all copies with the same weight matrices. Apart from the attention cache and the final MLP, no part of the Transformer depends on the streams.

Within one checkpoint, every stream shares the backbone weights, while its prefix and the aggregation MLP add a small number of parameters. Comparing different values of PPP is a different question. In the paper’s 42B-token scaling experiment, models at different PPP are trained separately on the same data budget, so their learned backbone weights need not be identical. This is a controlled comparison of training recipes, not an ablation that freezes one backbone and changes only repeated inference computation. The paper also tests that stricter frozen-backbone setting, under PEFT in §4.2.

The paper’s ablation experiments support this point. On the 0.5B model, the paper replaced three components:

  • Replacing the prefix with LoRA changes the loss by 0.0005 nats.
  • Replacing the learned weights with a plain average increases the loss by 0.0012 nats.
  • Replacing the MLP with a linear layer increases the loss by 0.0047 nats.

In comparison, each doubling of the stream count, from 2 to 4 and from 4 to 8, reduces the loss by about 0.013 nats. As long as the streams can differ from each other, the design of each component matters much less than the number of streams.

The streams inside a checkpoint: a small ensemble

To see what the streams do, I loaded the released 0.7B Pile checkpoints with P=1P=1P=1 and P=8P=8P=8 and ran the same 204-token text through both models.

The streams are clearly different from each other. On average, two streams predict the same top next token only 23% of the time, and for individual pairs of streams this rate ranges from 5% to 54%. The mean cosine similarity between the hidden states of two streams is 0.57.

Each stream alone is a weak model. The NLL of a single stream ranges from 4.05 to 6.62 nats per token, with a mean of 5.1, while the weighted combination of the 8 streams reaches 3.31. A plain average of the 8 streams reaches 3.34, almost as good as the weighted combination.

The learned weights do not select the best stream. The entropy of the weights is 2.026 nats, close to the maximum value of 2.079 for 8 streams, and the mean weight of each stream lies between 0.112 and 0.144. The stream with the highest mean weight (0.144) is in fact the weakest single stream on this text, with an NLL of 6.62. So the learned weights mix the streams almost evenly, and the smoothing during training encourages exactly this behavior.

To measure the contribution of each extra stream, I averaged every subset of mmm streams and scored each subset on the same text (Figure 1). Every subset is scored over all 203 predicted positions. A typical pair of streams reaches 4.07, four streams reach 3.58, and all eight streams reach 3.35. These numbers come from a CPU rerun of the same checkpoint, and its single-stream NLLs match the GPU run of the previous paragraph within 0.006 nats. Most of the gain therefore comes from the first few streams: going from four streams to eight streams improves the NLL by about 0.23 nats.

When only a few streams are kept, the choice of streams matters a lot. The best pair scores 3.51, and the worst pair scores 5.55. The learned weights do not use this difference. An oracle that picks the best stream for each token reaches 2.45, 0.87 nats lower than the learned weights, but the oracle uses the target token to choose a stream. This is a target-informed reference, not a measurement of deployable routing.

Figure 1: Mean next-token NLL over the 203 predicted positions of the 204-token text, for every subset of mmm streams. The band shows the range over the subsets of each size; with all eight streams there is only one subset. The dashed lines show the learned weights and the per-token oracle.

On this 204-token text, the streams work like an ensemble, not like a router; other texts and domains were not probed, so this is a description of one sample. Each stream is weaker than the combination and the errors of the streams overlap, and an ensemble helps exactly when the errors of its members are partly independent. The prefixes create the differences between the streams, while the remaining overlap limits how far the method can go. The paper models this overlap with a correlation coefficient ρ\rhoρ, and a later section discusses ρ\rhoρ in detail.

The scaling law: streams as extra parameters

Earlier scaling laws use two variables. Kaplan et al. (2020) found that the loss follows power laws in parameters, data and compute, and Hoffmann et al. (2022) proposed the form that most later work uses:

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ.L(N,D)=E+\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D^{\beta}}.L(N,D)=E+NαA​+DβB​.

Both laws describe training: a model with NNN parameters trained on DDD tokens uses about 6ND6ND6ND FLOPs and reaches a predictable loss. ParScale’s practical law adds a third variable in a simple way, by letting the number of streams multiply the parameter count:

Neff=N (1+kln⁡P),L(N,P)=(AN(1+kln⁡P))α+E.N_{\text{eff}} = N\,(1+k\ln P), \qquad L(N,P)=\left(\frac{A}{N(1+k\ln P)}\right)^{\alpha}+E.Neff​=N(1+klnP),L(N,P)=(N(1+klnP)A​)α+E.

When P=1P=1P=1, the factor in the brackets equals 1, and the law reduces to the parameter term of the Chinchilla law. The data term is merged into the constant because every run in the fit used the same 42B tokens, so the law describes only one amount of training data. Whether kkk changes with the amount of training data is still unknown, and the paper’s 1T-token run cannot answer this question because that run used a different training recipe.

The published loss tables contain six model sizes and four stream counts for each dataset. I refitted this form to the tables with least squares on log residuals and a Huber loss, and I computed the intervals from 400 bootstrap samples. The results are as follows:

  • On Stack-V2-Python, k=0.406k=0.406k=0.406 [0.362, 0.448]. So 8 streams are worth 1+kln⁡8=1.851+k\ln 8=1.851+kln8=1.85 [1.75, 1.93] times the parameters.
  • On Pile, k=0.346k=0.346k=0.346 [0.326, 0.363], and the multiplier is 1.72 [1.68, 1.76].

The paper reports k=0.393k=0.393k=0.393 and k=0.334k=0.334k=0.334 for the same tables. The small difference comes from the fitting objective and the starting points, and it changes the curves by only about 0.001 nats, so the logarithmic trend does not depend on one particular fitting choice.

Figure 2: Released loss points and the fitted log law for six model sizes on Stack-V2-Python and Pile. Each line is one model size. The points are the paper’s measurements.

Because of the logarithm, the gain from each new stream drops quickly. Each new stream is worth a share of the parameters, and since the derivative of N(1+kln⁡P)N(1+k\ln P)N(1+klnP) is kN/PkN/PkN/P, this share decreases as 1/P1/P1/P. On code, the gains are as follows:

  • Going from one stream to two streams increases the effective parameter count by 28%.
  • Going from four streams to eight streams increases the effective parameter count by another 18%.
  • The eighth stream alone adds about 3%.

If the law also held for larger PPP, the 1.6B model would need 78 streams on code and 167 streams on Pile to match the 4.4B model. These numbers suggest that ParScale is best used to make use of spare compute, not to replace a larger model.

The two ways of adding capacity can be compared in the same unit. Near P=8P=8P=8, one more stream increases the effective parameter count by about 5% and adds one forward pass per token, at a cost of about 2N2N2N FLOPs, whereas increasing the model size by 5% costs only about 0.1N0.1N0.1N FLOPs per token. Adding capacity with a stream therefore needs about 20 times more computation than adding capacity with parameters. This comparison counts nominal forward FLOPs rather than elapsed time. Whether the trade-off is useful depends on weight storage, KV space and the implementation cost measured in the inference section.

Two forms of the law

The paper also derives a second form from a model of the streams. This model treats the PPP streams as predictors whose relative errors have a correlation coefficient ρ\rhoρ, and it leads to the following form:

Neff=N(P(P−1)ρ+1)1/α.N_{\text{eff}} = N\left(\frac{P}{(P-1)\rho+1}\right)^{1/\alpha}.Neff​=N((P−1)ρ+1P​)1/α.

The value of ρ\rhoρ decides the behavior:

  • When ρ=1\rho=1ρ=1, the streams are exact copies and add nothing.
  • When ρ=0\rho=0ρ=0, the streams are independent, and the gain grows as P1/αP^{1/\alpha}P1/α.
  • When ρ\rhoρ is between 0 and 1, the gain stops growing at some point. As PPP increases, the multiplier approaches the upper limit ρ−1/α\rho^{-1/\alpha}ρ−1/α, and this upper limit depends on how much the streams still agree with each other.

The paper uses the log form as a simple substitute for this model because the measured points follow a logarithmic curve. Inside the measured range the two forms agree well, but outside it they behave differently: the log form keeps growing, while the correlation form approaches an upper limit.

I also fitted the correlation form, and it fits almost as well as the log form: R2=0.9957R^2=0.9957R2=0.9957 on Stack and R2=0.9965R^2=0.9965R2=0.9965 on Pile, with ρ=0.89\rho=0.89ρ=0.89 and ρ=0.90\rho=0.90ρ=0.90. According to the correlation form, the multiplier for 8 streams is 1.76 on Stack and 1.65 on Pile, and the upper limits are 1.92 and 1.77, so 8 streams already reach 92–93% of the maximum multiplier. The two curves cross near P=5.5P=5.5P=5.5, inside the measured range, where the difference between them is too small to show up in the loss. After P=8P=8P=8 the curves separate: at the 78 streams that the log law needs to match the 4.4B model, the log law is 44% higher than the upper limit of the correlation form.

The current data cannot decide between the two forms, because both forms fit the same 24 points and the largest measured stream count is only 8. The correlation form also rests on its own assumptions, for example that each stream follows a Chinchilla law and that one value of ρ\rhoρ applies to every PPP, and these assumptions may not hold for larger PPP. The log form, in contrast, assumes no mechanism at all. For PPP up to 8, the log form is simple and accurate; for larger PPP, the correlation form gives the more cautious estimate. A run with P=16P=16P=16 or P=32P=32P=32 on the same data would decide between the two forms.

Parallel streams are most similar to inference-time scaling, because both methods spend extra compute after the weights are fixed. Sampling methods spend this compute on more tokens or more candidate answers and then choose among the candidates with an external signal, such as a verifier, a majority vote or a unit test. ParScale instead spends the compute on more forward passes and learns how to combine them during training. As a result, sampling works with any checkpoint, while ParScale needs an extra training stage.

A mixture of experts (MoE) makes the opposite trade-off. MoE increases the total parameter count but activates only part of the parameters for each token, whereas ParScale keeps the parameter count the same and activates all parameters PPP times. In other words, MoE adds capacity per token and ParScale adds computation per token, and a model can use both methods at once.

Ensemble theory explains the upper limit. If MMM models have independent errors, averaging the MMM models reduces the variance by about 1/M1/M1/M. Because ParScale’s streams share all weights, their errors stay correlated, so the effective size of the ensemble is much smaller than PPP. The correlation form turns this idea into a formula.

On code, the 1.6B model with 8 streams and the 4.4B model have almost the same score. The validation loss shows a small difference, 1.0383 against 1.0213, and this gap of 0.017 nats is about what the law predicts: the paper’s own fit predicts 1.0410 and 1.0151, a gap of 0.026 nats in the same direction. On the general benchmarks the result is different again, because the 1.6B model with 8 streams scores 55.7, close to the 2.8B model (55.2) and below the 4.4B model (57.2).

These numbers are consistent with each other. A benchmark average is only a rough summary of a model’s ability, so two models can have the same average while their losses differ. There are therefore two conclusions:

  • On code benchmarks, the 1.6B model with 8 streams matches the 4.4B model.
  • In overall ability, the 1.6B model with 8 streams is closer to a model about 1.85 times larger, near the 2.8B model.

Both conclusions are useful, and the right one depends on the question.

The loss tables show one more pattern. For every model size, the first doubling of streams gives the largest single improvement: on Stack, going from one stream to two streams lowers the loss of the 1.6B model from 1.0817 to 1.0623, while the next two doublings together lower it by only another 0.024 nats. In the tables, smaller models gain more in absolute terms, but the relative gain is about the same for all sizes, and a multiplier of the form 1+kln⁡P1+k\ln P1+klnP predicts exactly this pattern. The paper reads its loss contour plots as showing that larger models benefit more, but the released tables alone do not confirm this reading.

The 1T-token results are the strongest evidence that the recipe works at a large scale. On GSM8K, the 1.8B model improves from 28.7 to 38.4, a relative gain of 34%, and on the math average it improves from 25.5 to 32.8. However, these gains do not come from the streams alone, because the two models received different training: the P=1P=1P=1 baseline is the stage-1 model, while the P=8P=8P=8 model also went through a second stage with 20B tokens of selected math, code and general data. A P=1P=1P=1 model trained with the same second stage would separate the two effects. At present, the clearest evidence for the streams is the 42B-token comparison, in which the data is the same and two streams reduce the loss by about 2%.

Inference cost

The paper estimates costs with a hardware model rather than with measurements, using the llm-analysis model of an A100 GPU at batch size 1. For the same performance gain, it finds that ParScale adds 22 times less memory and 6 times less latency than adding parameters, and these figures count only the memory added to the base model, not the total memory. This estimate is reasonable for comparing architectures, but the real cost depends on batch size, prompt length and hardware, so I also measured the cost directly.

I measured the two 0.7B checkpoints on an RTX 5070 Laptop with bf16 and SDPA attention. A request prefills 128 tokens, produces its first output token, and then runs exactly 32 cached one-token forward passes. It therefore produces 33 output tokens in total. EOS does not shorten the request. The throughput below counts only the 32 cached steps, not the first token from prefill.

Each point uses three warm-up requests and seven measured requests. Eight streams retain 78 to 91% of one stream’s median throughput in this implementation. The ratio is not monotonic in batch size, and the request ranges overlap in some comparisons. These points do not locate a compute-bound crossover or show that extra streams are free. The original difference-of-minima estimator is retired: it subtracted separate 32-token and 64-token generations and even produced negative prefill estimates. The figure and table now use explicit cached steps.

Sharing weights makes a bandwidth explanation plausible, but these timings do not measure weight traffic. A separate Nsight Systems trace of one warmed P=1P=1P=1, batch-1 request, with prefill and eight cached steps, records 14,482 CUDA kernels. The union of their execution intervals covers 67.665 ms of the 403.739 ms from the first kernel’s start to the last kernel’s end, about 16.8%. The remainder is not a measurement of GPU idle time or of Python overhead: copies, synchronization, scheduling and other activity must also be read from the timeline. This trace does show why a CUDA-event span should not be called kernel time, and it supplies no proof that weight bandwidth is the bottleneck. Its profiling duration does not replace the independent timings above.

Long-prompt work was measured by a different, historical protocol: batch 1, a full forward with no KV cache and logits for every prompt position. At 128 tokens that full forward took 60 ms with eight streams and 44 ms with one; at 2,048 tokens, 1.19 s and 152 ms. The longer forward exposes much more repeated computation, but it is not the same operation as the generation-style prefill above; that prefill keeps KV and computes only the last position’s logits.

KV storage is a separate cost. For this model, the cache of each stream uses 27.6 KB per token, because the model has 36 layers and two KV heads of dimension 96 and stores keys and values in bf16. With 8 streams and a 128-token context, the cache uses 28 MB, a small amount compared with the 1.34 GB of weights; with a 4,096-token context, the cache uses 0.9 GB, about two thirds of the weight size. The memory advantage therefore applies only to short contexts and small batches.

Training also has a cost. Training from scratch with 8 streams would need 8 times the FLOPs, and the paper avoids this cost with a two-stage recipe:

  1. Stage 1 pretrains a normal model on 1T tokens.
  2. Stage 2 trains the model with ParScale on 20B more tokens.

With P=8P=8P=8, stage 2 processes 160B stream-tokens, about 16% of the pretraining compute, while training a model 1.85 times larger from scratch on the same data would cost about 85% more. The two options are not fully comparable, however: the larger model stores its extra capacity in its weights, whereas ParScale pays for its extra capacity again at every inference.

Dynamic parallel scaling comes from a separate experiment in §4.2. The authors freeze a pretrained Qwen2.5-3B backbone and train only the introduced prefixes and aggregation parameters. Different adapters can then offer different values of PPP over the same backbone. This sharing is a property of that PEFT setup; the separately trained Pile checkpoints measured above are not interchangeable adapters over identical backbone weights.

Where the method is useful

For scaling-law research, ParScale’s main contribution is a new controlled variable. Earlier laws describe the loss with NNN and DDD, while ParScale adds PPP with NNN and DDD held fixed, and its formula has only one or two parameters (kkk and ρ\rhoρ) that a small grid of experiments can estimate. This design is valuable even if the exact formula changes later.

The upper limit of the correlation form connects the law to ensemble ideas: averaging helps more when errors are less correlated. The measured streams still overlap in their predictions; sharing weights alone does not prove a universal correlation floor. This upper limit can also be tested. If the multiplier at P=16P=16P=16 and P=32P=32P=32 approaches 1.92, the correlation form is the better description; if the multiplier keeps growing along the log curve, the log form is the better description.

For serving, weight sharing saves parameter storage, while each extra stream still adds computation and KV state. Small-batch, memory-constrained deployments may benefit from that trade-off. The direct measurements above describe one reference Python loop on short contexts; they do not establish a bandwidth bottleneck or the batch size at which an optimized serving engine would become compute-bound.

The two-stage recipe reduces the cost of adding parallel streams after ordinary pretraining. The frozen-backbone PEFT experiment goes further and shows that the added parameters can provide several compute levels over one backbone. These are distinct training settings, and a deployment comparison has to keep the adapter training, decoding cost and KV space attached to the setting that produced them.

Conclusion

ParScale adds a third variable to the scaling laws and gives this variable a formula. With one stream, the formula reduces to the parameter term of the Chinchilla law; with more streams, each stream is worth a share of the parameters, and this share decreases as 1/P1/P1/P. The parameter-equivalent gain and the serving cost answer different questions: the refit describes loss, while the measured cost depends on the implementation, context, batch size and hardware.

The law has two forms. The log form is accurate inside the measured range and keeps growing outside it, while the correlation form fits almost as well and stops growing at ρ−1/α\rho^{-1/\alpha}ρ−1/α. The current data fit both forms, and a run with more streams would show which form is correct.

On code benchmarks, the 1.6B model with 8 streams matches the 4.4B model, but in loss and in the general average it is closer to the 2.8B model. Anyone can check these results on a laptop because the authors released their checkpoints and loss tables, and this release deserves credit.

Parallel streams are a real third way to scale a model: they add effective parameters at a logarithmic rate, and the size of the gain depends on how different the streams can be.

The ensemble statistics were recomputed on CPU (bf16, PyTorch 2.6.0, transformers 4.51.3) after a correction to ensemble_curve.py: each subset is now scored over all 203 predicted positions, and the range is taken across subsets. The first version mixed the variation between text chunks into the range and weighted the short last chunk like a full one. The text is 204 tokens from an essay on the history of computing, tokenized with the checkpoint’s own tokenizer. A separate GPU rerun checks all 255 subsets against the preserved CPU result; the maximum NLL difference is 0.00717 nats, below the predeclared 0.02-nat numerical tolerance. Figure 1 retains the CPU data.
  • Prefill timing uses prefill_latency.py: batch size 1, a full forward pass without a KV cache, and the median of seven runs after two warm-up runs.
  • Direct decode timing uses decode_latency.py: a 128-token prefill and 32 cached steps, three warm-up requests and seven measured requests per point. Every saved sample includes per-step CUDA-event durations and full-request wall time. Model loading and tokenization are outside timing. The environment is Python 3.13.5, PyTorch 2.14.1+cu130, transformers 4.51.3, einops 0.8.2 and driver 610.88. The script records source and checkpoint hashes, and checks finite logits and agreement with greedy generation before timing.
  • The public reproduction package contains the scripts, raw per-request results, figure entry points and pinned checkpoint revisions. results/decode_p1.json and decode_p8.json carry the direct timing evidence; decode_trace_summary.json is a path-free summary tied to the SHA-256 of the original Nsight Systems report. Model weights are downloaded separately. The original report stays in the measurement archive because it contains host details.

The fit starts from the paper’s parameters and from a coarse grid. On Pile, a fit that started only from the grid reached a worse solution (R2R^2R2 0.983). For this reason, the published values are used as starting points. The reported intervals come from the bootstrap, not from this choice.
