Serial and Parallel Selective-Scan Kernels Compared on a Laptop GPU

A time-serial scan was fastest in 11 of 12 shapes after its initialization was synchronized, and tied in the twelfth. This post compares six CUDA implementations, tests their allocation cost and varies the number of chains.

I timed six hand-written CUDA kernels for Mamba’s selective scan on an RTX 5070 Laptop GPU. Four use chunked parallel scans to break the dependency on time, while two process the sequence one step at a time. After fixing a cross-lane initialization hazard in the warp implementation, the serial version remained fastest in 11 of 12 shapes. At 8,192 steps, ssm.cu took 3.035 ms, and the four chunked implementations took 6.030 to 15.245 ms.

Removing a dependency costs work: more launches, scratch traffic and, in two implementations, atomic accumulation. For these implementations and the original shape grid, that work usually cost more than it saved. Only at the narrowest state, N=32N=32N=32, did a chunked implementation have a lower median, and its three runs overlapped those of the serial kernel, so that case is a tie within run-to-run variation. The experiments below also vary the number of independent chains and move allocation outside the timed call, so those explanations can be checked separately.

The post first defines the operator and the one algebraic fact that every parallel version relies on, with a two-step example that can be checked by hand. It then walks through the three designs and compares them with Mamba’s own kernel. Along the way, it explains a “bug” that I first thought one of the files had and why it turned out not to be a bug. The last part presents the measurements and two control experiments.

Fundamentals: a recurrence with a data-dependent decay

The scan takes six inputs and produces one output. For a batch of BBB sequences, sequence length LLL, model width DDD and state width NNN:

u, Δ, y∈RB×L×D,A∈RD×N,B,C∈RB×L×N,skip∈RD.u,\ \Delta,\ y\in\mathbb R^{B\times L\times D},\qquad A\in\mathbb R^{D\times N},\qquad B,C\in\mathbb R^{B\times L\times N},\qquad \text{skip}\in\mathbb R^{D}.u, Δ, y∈RB×L×D,A∈RD×N,B,C∈RB×L×N,skip∈RD.

For every (b, t, d, n) the two coefficient arrays are

at,d,n=exp⁡(Δt,d Ad,n),bt,d,n=Δt,d ut,d Bt,n,a_{t,d,n}=\exp(\Delta_{t,d}\,A_{d,n}),\qquad b_{t,d,n}=\Delta_{t,d}\,u_{t,d}\,B_{t,n},at,d,n​=exp(Δt,d​Ad,n​),bt,d,n​=Δt,d​ut,d​Bt,n​,

and the state and output are

ht,d,n=at,d,n ht−1,d,n+bt,d,n,h−1,d,n=0,h_{t,d,n}=a_{t,d,n}\,h_{t-1,d,n}+b_{t,d,n},\qquad h_{-1,d,n}=0,ht,d,n​=at,d,n​ht−1,d,n​+bt,d,n​,h−1,d,n​=0, yt,d=∑n=0N−1Ct,n ht,d,n+skipd ut,d.y_{t,d}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}C_{t,n}\,h_{t,d,n}+\text{skip}_d\,u_{t,d}.yt,d​=n=0∑N−1​Ct,n​ht,d,n​+skipd​ut,d​.

These formulas define the whole operator. The files contain no input projection, causal convolution, softplus on Δ\DeltaΔ, gate or backward pass, because they implement only the inner scan of a Mamba block, not the whole block.

Each output needs the state from the previous step, and this state is scaled by a decay that depends on the current input. So hth_tht​ cannot be computed before ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​ is known. Every design below is a different answer to one question: how much of this step-by-step walk can be avoided?

The one fact parallel scans rely on

One step is an affine map, h↦a h+bh\mapsto a\,h+bh↦ah+b, and the composition of two affine maps is again an affine map. A chunk of steps is therefore one map h↦α h+βh\mapsto \alpha\,h+\betah↦αh+β. Two chunks, an earlier “left” chunk and a later “right” chunk, combine as

α=αright αleft,β=αright βleft+βright.\alpha=\alpha_{\text{right}}\,\alpha_{\text{left}},\qquad \beta=\alpha_{\text{right}}\,\beta_{\text{left}}+\beta_{\text{right}}.α=αright​αleft​,β=αright​βleft​+βright​.

This rule can be checked by hand with two identical steps, a=0.5a=0.5a=0.5 and b=1b=1b=1, starting from h=0h=0h=0. Step by step, h=0.5⋅0+1=1h=0.5\cdot0+1=1h=0.5⋅0+1=1 and then h=0.5⋅1+1=1.5h=0.5\cdot1+1=1.5h=0.5⋅1+1=1.5. With the combined map, α=0.5⋅0.5=0.25\alpha=0.5\cdot0.5=0.25α=0.5⋅0.5=0.25 and β=0.5⋅1+1=1.5\beta=0.5\cdot1+1=1.5β=0.5⋅1+1=1.5, so the chunk maps 000 to 0.25⋅0+1.5=1.50.25\cdot0+1.5=1.50.25⋅0+1.5=1.5. Both ways give the same answer.

Because this combination is associative, the (α,β)(\alpha,\beta)(α,β) of every chunk can be computed in parallel, and a parallel prefix scan can then combine them. One more consequence matters later. Since the scan always starts from h−1=0h_{-1}=0h−1​=0, the state at the start of any chunk is just the β\betaβ of all earlier chunks combined, and the α\alphaα of the earlier chunks only ever multiplies zero.

Three designs for one recurrence

The six files give three answers to the same question, with two files for each answer:

  1. Keep time serial, parallelize the state. One warp or block owns a (batch, d_model) pair, keeps the NNN states on chip and loops over time: ssm.cu and selective_scan_rewrite.cu.
  2. Chunked scan with atomics. The kernels compute the (α,β)(\alpha,\beta)(α,β) of each chunk, scan the chunk summaries into prefixes, replay each chunk from its prefix and use atomicAdd to add the contribution of each state into yyy: ssm_seq.cu and selective_scan_improved.cu.
  3. Chunked scan with a deterministic reduction. The decomposition is the same, but the contributions go to a buffer, and a second kernel sums the buffer in a fixed order. As a result, the output does not depend on which atomic operation finishes first: selective_scan_seq_parallel.cu and selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu.

Figure 2: The three designs. The first keeps time inside one block; the other two expose per-chunk summaries and combine them across blocks.

What “parallel” has to achieve

The time-serial design has B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D independent chains. Each chain is LLL steps long, and each step updates NNN states. In the shapes I measured, B⋅DB\cdot DB⋅D is between 64 and 512, so even without any scan technique there are 64 to 512 chains for the 36 SMs of the GPU. Each step updates the NNN states and reduces their contributions, and this work must finish before the next step can start.

A parallel scan replaces this dependency with more work: chunk-local scans, a prefix scan, a replay, and either atomic operations or a reduction buffer. The question is whether this extra work costs less than the dependency it removes.

Design 1: one chain per warp or block

ssm.cu has four kernels. Each kernel assigns a warp or a block to each chain, and it keeps the state in shared memory or, when the state does not fit, in a device buffer. solve uses the warp kernel when N≤64N\le64N≤64 and the block kernel when NNN is larger, so most shapes here use the warp kernel with shared state. This excerpt shows its vectorized time loop, omitting setup and the scalar fallback for state widths that are not divisible by four:

for (int t = 0; t < seq_len; ++t) {
  // load u, delta, B, C for this (b, t, d)
  float local_sum = 0.f;
  for (int i = lane; i < d_state / 4; i += 32) {   // one float4 of states per lane
    float4 hv = h4[i];
    local_sum += update_state4(hv, a4[i], b4[i], c4[i], delta_val, delta_u);
    h4[i] = hv;
  }
  const float sum = warp_reduce_sum(local_sum);
  if (lane == 0) y[udy] = sum + skip_d * u_val;
}

update_state4 is the arithmetic core. It updates four states in one float4, with one expf and one FMA for each state, and adds their share of C⋅hC\cdot hC⋅h. Each lane handles one float4, so at N=64N=64N=64 only sixteen of the 32 lanes do work, and at N=32N=32N=32 only eight lanes do. The state never leaves the chip, and the only global write in each step is one value of yyy.

The measured binary’s warp-shared kernel has 480 addressed SASS instructions, including 10 SHFL, 67 FFMA and 12 MUFU instructions. The earlier count of 958 included instruction-encoding continuation lines. These are static instruction sites, not dynamic execution counts.

One warp owning a chain does not itself order memory accesses between lanes. Lane jjj initially zeroes h[j]h[j]h[j], h[j+32]h[j+32]h[j+32], …, while lane iii later reads h[4i..4i+3]h[4i..4i+3]h[4i..4i+3] as one float4. The corrected source places __syncwarp() between initialization and the first read, as required for this exchange by the CUDA Programming Guide. The same ordering was added to the warp-global fallback. Inside the time loop, each lane owns its float4, so this initialization barrier is outside the repeated work. The historical source now reproduces a shared-memory RAW hazard in Racecheck: a small B=1,L=33,D=7,N=32B=1,L=33,D=7,N=32B=1,L=33,D=7,N=32 case reported 1,736 warnings. The corrected source reported zero hazards on that case, and zero memory, race and synchronization errors across the twelve checked warp and block cases.

The main table below uses the corrected implementation. A separate interleaved run compared the corrected and the historical source on the twelve shapes. Their median-time ratios ranged from 0.845 to 1.134 in both directions, and these differences lie within the run-to-run variation of this laptop: the corrected kernel took 3.603 ms at L=8192L=8192L=8192 in that run and 3.035 ms in the main run. The comparison therefore shows that the barrier does not change the scaling, but it cannot measure the cost of one barrier outside the loop. The unsynchronized historical source remains an audit baseline, not a supported implementation.

At B=1,D=64B=1,D=64B=1,D=64, the launch exposes 64 independent warp-sized blocks to 36 SMs. Increasing LLL adds dependent iterations within each block; increasing BDB DBD instead adds independent blocks. Their measured scaling can distinguish these two kinds of work, but static SASS alone cannot establish which hardware stall dominates.

selective_scan_rewrite.cu follows the same idea, but it assigns one block to each chain, keeps the state in registers and uses a block-wide reduction. This reduction needs shuffles across all warps of the block and barriers in every step: its SASS contains 45 SHFL and 6 BAR.SYNC instructions.

Design 2: chunked scan with atomics

ssm_seq.cu and selective_scan_improved.cu share one design with two paths.

Short sequences (L≤1024L\le1024L≤1024). One block per (b, d, n) covers the whole sequence. The block has next_pow2(L) threads, and a Hillis–Steele scan in shared memory combines the (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) pairs of the steps. Every thread then uses atomicAdd to add C · h for its step into yyy. Before that, a separate kernel writes skip · u into yyy. At L=1024L=1024L=1024 with the default shape, this path launches 4,096 blocks of 1,024 threads.

Long sequences. The sequence is split into chunks of 256 steps, and four kernels run one after another:

  1. write skip · u into yyy;
  2. scan each chunk, store the local (a,b)(a, b)(a,b) pair of every step, and write the summary (α,β)(\alpha, \beta)(α,β) of the chunk;
  3. scan the chunk summaries of each state into exclusive prefixes;
  4. replay each step from the prefix of its chunk, h = local_a · prefix + local_b, and use atomicAdd to add C · h into yyy.

This path needs one atomic operation per state and time step. At L=8192L=8192L=8192 with D=N=64D=N=64D=N=64, there are 4,096 states and 8,192 steps, so the path performs about 33.5 million atomic operations, 64 for each output element. The stored local pairs take 2×4096×8192×42\times4096\times8192\times42×4096×8192×4 bytes, or 256 MiB. Step 2 writes them and step 4 reads them back, although the inputs of the whole operator total only 8 MiB.

The bug that wasn’t

ssm_seq.cu keeps only the β\betaβ half of each prefix, while selective_scan_improved.cu keeps both halves. A comment in selective_scan_improved.cu says that keeping α\alphaα “fixes the old code’s loss of affine scale information”. I first read this comment as a sign of a correctness bug in ssm_seq.cu that the test data happened to hide.

It is not a bug. As Figure 1 shows, the scan starts from h−1=0h_{-1}=0h−1​=0, so the α\alphaα of the prefix only ever multiplies zero. Both files replay with exactly the same expression, local_a · prefix_b + local_b, and selective_scan_improved.cu reads its stored prefix_a only to discard it ((void)prefix_a;). The code comment also explains why the value is kept: the kernel may later accept a non-zero initial state. This is a reasonable interface choice, not a fix. The two files compute the same operator, and their errors agree.

The real difference between the two files is memory management. In every call, ssm_seq.cu allocates its four scratch buffers with cudaMalloc and releases them with cudaFree, and cudaFree waits for the whole device. selective_scan_improved.cu instead computes the size of one workspace and allocates it with the stream-ordered cudaMallocAsync/cudaFreeAsync, and these functions reuse memory from the driver’s pool. It can also accept a workspace owned by the caller.

For sequences longer than 1,024 chunks (262,144 steps), selective_scan_improved.cu falls back to a kernel that recomputes every output from time zero. This fallback needs O(L2)O(L^2)O(L2) work and exists only as a correctness fallback.

Design 3: chunked scan with a deterministic reduction

selective_scan_seq_parallel.cu runs the same chunk scan and prefix scan. Instead of adding into yyy, it writes the contribution of each state into a buffer, contrib[state, t], and a final kernel sums the NNN contributions of each output in a fixed order. Its workspace needs 12 bytes per state and step, for the local aaa, the local bbb and the contribution. At L=8192L=8192L=8192, the workspace is 384 MiB.

This file has a fixed limit. MAX_CHUNKS allows 4,096 chunks, but the prefix kernel uses one thread per chunk and limits its block to 1,024 threads. So a sequence longer than 1024×256=262,1441024\times256=262{,}1441024×256=262,144 steps would get prefixes for only its first 1,024 chunks. No shape here comes close to this limit, but very long contexts would reach it.

selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu does not store the pairs of each step at all:

  1. scan each chunk and keep only its summary;
  2. scan the summaries in two levels, first within groups of chunks and then across groups;
  3. for a tile of 32 states at a time, re-run each chunk from its prefix and write a partial output for each tile;
  4. sum the tiles and add skip · u.

Its only large buffer is the partial output, with one copy per state tile. At N=64N=64N=64, this buffer is twice the output size. Among the four parallel designs, this file uses the least memory.

What Mamba’s own kernel does

The reference kernel in mamba_ssm runs the whole scan in one launch and never writes chunk summaries to global memory.

flowchart TD
    G["one thread block per (batch, channel)"] --> CH["for each chunk of time steps, in order"]
    CH --> ST["for each state n"]
    ST --> SC["block-wide scan over the chunk's steps: each thread holds consecutive steps, CUB combines their (a, b) pairs"]
    SC --> PF["keep the chunk's last state as the next chunk's starting prefix"]
    SC --> ACC["add C·h into this chunk's outputs, held in registers"]
    ACC --> W["after the state loop: write this chunk's y"]
    PF -. "next chunk" .-> CH

Figure 3: Mamba’s fused forward kernel. It is parallel over time within a chunk, serial over chunks and states, and carries the prefix in shared memory.

So Mamba does parallelize over time. It uses the same affine scan as design 2, but it runs the scan inside one block: the block processes the chunks in order and carries the running prefix from one chunk to the next. Because no data crosses blocks, the kernel needs no atomic operations, no scratch arrays and no extra launches.

How I measured

All six files expose the same solve(...) entry point. A shared harness generates deterministic inputs and first compares two calls with an independent double-precision CPU recurrence. Before each check it poisons the output, rejects NaN/Inf and requires every element to satisfy ∣y−yref∣≤2×10−5+2×10−4∣yref∣|y-y_{\rm ref}|\le2\times10^{-5}+2\times10^{-4}|y_{\rm ref}|∣y−yref​∣≤2×10−5+2×10−4∣yref​∣. Any CUDA error, unchecked shape or failed comparison exits with an error instead of emitting a performance result.

After validation, six calls warm the implementation. CUDA events enclose 30 solve calls, and a host clock measures the same batch through completion. The reported device-timeline interval includes whatever happens between those events, including gaps while the host allocates or submits work; it is not the sum of kernel durations. Inputs and final output copies lie outside the timed region. There are three independent processes per cell, with variant order interleaved between rounds. The CSVs retain each round; the table uses their medians and the plot shades their minimum-to-maximum range.

Inputs are uniform in [−0.5,0.5][-0.5,0.5][−0.5,0.5], except Δ∈[0.01,0.06]\Delta\in[0.01,0.06]Δ∈[0.01,0.06] and A∈[−0.6,−0.1]A\in[-0.6,-0.1]A∈[−0.6,−0.1], so each decay lies in [0.965,0.999][0.965,0.999][0.965,0.999]. The main timing grid uses seed 17. A separate correctness grid covers 13 boundary shapes, three seeds and all six implementations: 234 cases, each checked twice. It includes odd state widths, partial chunks, L=1023/1024/1025L=1023/1024/1025L=1023/1024/1025 and the warp-to-block change at N=64/65N=64/65N=64/65.

The GPU is an RTX 5070 Laptop (8 GiB, SM 120). Laptop clocks and host submission vary between processes, so the ranges matter when implementations are close. Measurements were run serially, with no other experimental GPU workload running.

What the original shape grid supports

ssm.cu wins 11 of the 12 rows. The largest measured ratio is 24.85×, between the streaming implementation at 5.364 ms and ssm.cu at 0.216 ms for B=8,L=512B=8,L=512B=8,L=512. This compares complete solve interfaces, so the ratio includes allocation, initialization and all launches of each implementation.

The serial implementation takes 0.098 ms at L=256L=256L=256 and 3.035 ms at L=8192L=8192L=8192: 32 times the iterations cost 31.0 times the time. At L=1024L=1024L=1024, increasing DDD from 64 to 128 and 256 costs 0.394, 0.386 and 0.468 ms. The non-monotonic middle value is another reason not to infer a precise occupancy threshold from three medians. This grid is consistent with long dependent chains plus room for more independent blocks; it does not measure hardware stalls directly.

At N=32N=32N=32, selective_scan_improved.cu has the lower median, 0.318 ms against 0.391 ms for the serial implementation. The three runs do not separate them: the improved implementation ranged from 0.306 to 0.475 ms, the serial one from 0.391 to 0.448 ms, and the serial kernel took 0.457 ms at this shape in the separate synchronization run. This is the only row in which the winner’s slowest run is slower than the runner-up’s fastest run, so it is a tie on this setup, not a demonstrated win of the chunked design. In every other row, the ranges of the winner and the runner-up do not overlap.

What allocation could explain

On long sequences, ssm_seq.cu takes 4.336–15.245 ms and the improved implementation 1.774–6.030 ms. At L=8192L=8192L=8192, the ratio is 2.53×. Both allocate scratch memory, but the former uses four synchronous allocations and frees while the latter uses stream-ordered allocation. This is a candidate explanation, not something the full-interface comparison isolates. The allocation-only experiment below keeps the kernels fixed and moves their workspace ownership to the caller.

The shorter path also matters: at L=256L=256L=256, the improved implementation has a 0.224 ms median and ssm_seq.cu 0.144 ms, while at L=512L=512L=512 they are close (0.264 vs. 0.259 ms). Allocation is not present on that path and cannot explain those differences. They remain interface/compiler/runtime comparisons rather than a single-variable experiment.

Conclusion

A synchronized time-serial implementation remains fastest in 11 of the original 12 shapes. At the narrowest state, the best chunked implementation ties with it within run-to-run variation, while the full-interface ratios elsewhere reflect extra launches, scratch storage, replay and reduction. The two control experiments separate allocation from kernel work and show how changing the number of independent chains changes the comparison.

A parallel scan is useful when shortening the dependent chain repays the extra work of the chosen implementation; the crossover must be measured for the actual shape and interface.

     -D_ALLOW_COMPILER_AND_STL_VERSION_MISMATCH \
     -c selective_scan.cu -o selective_scan.obj

  • Timing: CUDA-event and host-wall intervals around 30 solve calls after 6 warm-ups; median and full range of 3 interleaved rounds. Correctness checks precede timing.

  • Reference: an independent CPU double-precision recurrence; finite-value checks and per-element absolute-plus-relative tolerance. Raw errors are retained.

  • Profiling: short Nsight Systems CUDA traces verify allocation and launch counts. A new NCU attempt returned ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM; no driver settings were changed and no hardware-stall percentage is inferred. SASS counts come from the measured binaries via cuobjdump --dump-sass, counting addressed instructions only.

Source

All six kernels are in Murmurwkk/cuda-operators, selective_scan/:

  • ssm.cu: warp or block per chain, shared or global state; solve picks the variant.
  • selective_scan_rewrite.cu: one block per chain, register state, block reduction.
  • ssm_seq.cu: short-path scan and chunked scan with atomics.
  • selective_scan_improved.cu: the same design with a workspace API and stream-ordered allocation.
  • selective_scan_seq_parallel.cu: stored contributions and a fixed-order reduction.
  • selective_scan_streaming_seq_parallel_fixed.cu: chunk summaries, two-level prefix, recompute per state tile, tile reduction.

The benchmark package contains the harness, build/run commands, raw rounds, summaries, sanitizer logs, source/binary SHA-256 identities and SASS counts. plot_timings.py reads these CSVs. The historical unsynchronized source is labelled unsafe and requires explicit opt-in for the audit comparison.
