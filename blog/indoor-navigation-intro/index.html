Indoor Navigation Basics: Dead Reckoning, SE(2) Geometry, WiFi Fingerprints, and Pose Graphs

An introductory guide to indoor positioning and navigation showing how geometry from SLAM, statistical signal models like RSSI and WKNN, Kalman filtering, and factor-graph optimization work together.

Outdoor navigation relies on satellite signals (GNSS/GPS) to measure absolute position directly. Inside buildings, underground stations, or urban canyons, satellite signals are blocked or heavily reflected, so an indoor navigation system must fuse imperfect clues borrowed from several disciplines: rigid-body geometry from robotics and SLAM, statistical signal modeling for radio fingerprints, recursive filtering from control theory, and nonlinear least-squares optimization. This guide introduces those building blocks in order—from walking dead reckoning and SE(2)SE(2)SE(2) planar poses, through RSSI fingerprints and WKNN, to Kalman filtering and pose-graph optimization.

Readers looking for the deeper mathematical derivations and experimental studies on this site can continue with the navigation reading guide and glossary, SE(2) local correction, Student-t motion noise, WiFi fingerprint map density, and QR marginalization. All small arithmetic examples worked directly in the text are marked (computed).

The core tension: relative motion vs. absolute observations

Almost every positioning algorithm balances two complementary information sources:

  • Relative motion measurements (dead reckoning): Wheel encoders, inertial measurement units (IMUs), or pedestrian step-and-heading counters track how far and in which direction the user moved since the previous instant. They are smooth and accurate over a few seconds, but small errors accumulate without bound (drift).
  • Absolute position clues: Radio signals (WiFi, Bluetooth Low Energy, UWB), visual landmarks, barometers, or magnetic profiles tie the user back to a known map. Individual readings are noisy and sometimes ambiguous, yet their error does not grow with travel time.

Planar geometry and SE(2): why pose is not a plain vector

A pedestrian or wheeled robot moving on a floor has two position coordinates (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) and one heading angle θ\thetaθ. Treating (x,y,θ)(x, y, \theta)(x,y,θ) as an ordinary Euclidean vector breaks down immediately for two reasons.

First, heading wraps around on a circle: turning 2∘2^\circ2∘ from 359∘359^\circ359∘ produces 1∘1^\circ1∘, not 361∘361^\circ361∘, and the shortest angular difference between 359∘359^\circ359∘ and 1∘1^\circ1∘ is 2∘2^\circ2∘, not 358∘358^\circ358∘. Second, translation and rotation do not commute. Start at (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0) facing East (θ=0∘\theta = 0^\circθ=0∘):

  • Walk 2 m, then turn left 90∘90^\circ90∘: you end at (2,0)(2, 0)(2,0) facing North (θ=90∘\theta = 90^\circθ=90∘).
  • Turn left 90∘90^\circ90∘, then walk 2 m: you end at (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2) facing North (θ=90∘\theta = 90^\circθ=90∘).

Both paths share the same final heading, yet end at completely different positions. To compose motions cleanly, robotics represents a 2D pose as an element of the Special Euclidean group SE(2)SE(2)SE(2), written as a 3×33 \times 33×3 homogeneous transformation matrix:

T=(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θxsin⁡θcos⁡θy001)T = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & x \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}T=​cosθsinθ0​−sinθcosθ0​xy1​​

Composing a relative step ΔT\Delta TΔT onto the current pose TkT_kTk​ is simply matrix multiplication Tk+1=TkΔTT_{k+1} = T_k \Delta TTk+1​=Tk​ΔT, which automatically rotates the local step into the global frame using the current heading θk\theta_kθk​.

That coupling explains why heading errors dominate dead-reckoning drift. Radio fingerprints: RSSI and WKNN

To cancel accumulated drift, indoor systems often observe Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) values from nearby WiFi access points (APs), measured in dBm (negative numbers where −45 dBm-45\text{ dBm}−45 dBm is much stronger than −85 dBm-85\text{ dBm}−85 dBm).

Because indoor walls, furniture, and moving people cause reflection and attenuation (multipath and shadowing), converting RSSI straight into distance via a free-space path-loss formula is usually unreliable. Instead, fingerprinting builds a site survey map recording the vector of observed AP signal strengths at known reference locations. At runtime, the system compares the live scan against the map.

The simplest statistical matcher is Weighted kkk-Nearest Neighbors (WKNN). Suppose a live scan from two APs reads z=(−55,−70) dBmz = (-55, -70)\text{ dBm}z=(−55,−70) dBm, and the map contains two reference points:

  • Point BBB at (10,0) m(10, 0)\text{ m}(10,0) m with stored vector (−63,−76) dBm(-63, -76)\text{ dBm}(−63,−76) dBm: signal distance dB=(−55+63)2+(−70+76)2=64+36=10 dBd_B = \sqrt{(-55 + 63)^2 + (-70 + 76)^2} = \sqrt{64 + 36} = 10\text{ dB}dB​=(−55+63)2+(−70+76)2​=64+36​=10 dB (computed).

WKNN is simple and non-parametric, but its normalized weights do not represent true localization uncertainty. If two symmetric corridors on opposite sides of a wall happen to produce similar RSSI distances (dA=dBd_A = d_BdA​=dB​), WKNN assigns equal weights and averages the estimate right into the solid wall between them. Probabilistic models—such as Gaussian distributions per location or Gaussian Process (GP) spatial signal fields—improve on WKNN by modeling both the expected signal and its variance across space.

Sensor fusion: recursive filtering vs. pose-graph optimization

Once both relative motion and noisy absolute measurements are available, two major estimation paradigms combine them.

Recursive filtering (Kalman filter intuition)

A Kalman filter maintains only the current state (mean estimate x^\hat{x}x^ and covariance PPP) in an alternating loop:

  1. Predict: propagate the state forward with dead reckoning; because motion has noise, uncertainty PPP grows.
  2. Update: blend the prediction with an incoming absolute observation using an optimal gain KKK weighted by relative precision.

Consider a 1D example: dead reckoning predicts x^−=10.0 m\hat{x}^- = 10.0\text{ m}x^−=10.0 m with variance P−=4.0 m2P^- = 4.0\text{ m}^2P−=4.0 m2 (σ=2.0 m\sigma = 2.0\text{ m}σ=2.0 m), while a WiFi measurement reports z=13.0 mz = 13.0\text{ m}z=13.0 m with variance R=1.0 m2R = 1.0\text{ m}^2R=1.0 m2 (σ=1.0 m\sigma = 1.0\text{ m}σ=1.0 m). The Kalman gain is:

K=P−P−+R=4.04.0+1.0=0.8(computed)K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + R} = \frac{4.0}{4.0 + 1.0} = 0.8 \quad \text{(computed)}K=P−+RP−​=4.0+1.04.0​=0.8(computed)

Because 2D/3D rotations are nonlinear, real navigation systems use the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) (linearizing around the current estimate) or Particle Filters (representing multimodal beliefs with weighted hypotheses, useful when corridor ambiguity cannot be approximated by a single Gaussian).

Pose-graph / factor-graph optimization

Filtering updates only the present moment; once past states are discarded, a later landmark or loop closure cannot retroactively straighten an earlier segment of the trajectory.

A factor graph (pose graph) keeps a window or the full sequence of poses {T0,T1,…,Tn}\{T_0, T_1, \dots, T_n\}{T0​,T1​,…,Tn​} as nodes. Every measurement becomes a factor (an elastic spring) connecting the poses it constrains: odometry factors link consecutive poses (Tk,Tk+1)(T_k, T_{k+1})(Tk​,Tk+1​), WiFi or anchor factors attach unary springs to individual poses, and loop-closure factors connect non-adjacent poses when the user revisits a recognized location.

Real-world failure modes worth knowing early

Textbook equations assume clean Gaussian noise and independent trials, while real indoor data breaks both assumptions regularly:

  • Heavy-tailed outliers: A sudden WiFi reflection, wrong floor detection, or pocket-induced IMU spike produces a huge residual. Squared error amplifies outliers quadratically and can yank the whole trajectory off course; practical estimators replace quadratic penalties with robust M-estimators (such as Huber, Cauchy, or Student-ttt likelihoods) that cap the influence of extreme residuals.
  • Unobservability and calibration: Certain errors (for example, a constant magnetometer bias or unknown initial yaw without external anchors) cannot be identified from motion alone without sufficient excitation or complementary sensors.
  • Evaluation leakage: Random train/test splits of a walking log place temporally adjacent scans into both sets, making a model look far more accurate than it will perform on a brand-new trajectory. Rigorous navigation benchmarks evaluate on held-out routes or buildings.
