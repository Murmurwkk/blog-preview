Scaling Laws: How the Compute-Optimal Training Recipe Changes

This note explains how the compute-optimal training recipe follows from scaling laws, and how finite data, coupled terms, serving cost and inference compute change it. With it, a reader can redo the choice for a new budget.

Scaling laws predict how the loss of a language model depends on its parameters, its training data and its compute, so they let a team choose a model size before it spends a training budget. The recipe that these laws give has changed several times, and most of the changes came from new conditions rather than from errors in the earlier laws. This note covers how “better” becomes a number, why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed, how to solve for the optimum under a fixed budget, and how four conditions move the answer: finite data, coupling between parameters and data, the cost of serving, and compute at inference time. All fitted results come from the papers at the linked versions; my own recomputations from their tables and saved fits are marked (computed).

The question the laws answer

Training a 7B model on 140B tokens costs roughly the same compute as training a 70B model on 14B tokens.

The difference lies elsewhere. A bigger model can hold more knowledge, while a smaller model can read more data for the same budget. Trial and error is also expensive here, because training both big models would use up the budget. The value of scaling laws is that, after a few small experiments, they allow a prediction about this choice before the budget is spent.

However, one curve of “how much better per order of magnitude of parameters” is not enough. The two configurations above change the parameters and the training data at the same time. If the model will be deployed heavily, the size of the weights matters, and if the corpus has run out, reading it again is not the same as getting a new one. The recipes in the literature keep changing, and most of the reasons lie in these conditions.

Making “better” a number

Language model training is usually judged by the validation cross-entropy: the higher the probability assigned to the correct next token, the smaller the negative log-probability, averaged over many tokens. This metric is smoother than accuracy of the type “must answer the whole question correctly”, so it shows scaling regularities more easily. The cost is that the average prediction loss and the accuracy on a given task are not the same objective.

Let NNN be the number of dense model parameters and DDD the total number of tokens processed in training. When the compute is dominated by the matrix multiplications of the parameters, the training FLOPs are commonly estimated as

C≈6ND.C\approx6ND.C≈6ND.

The forward pass costs about 2N2N2N FLOPs per token and the backward pass about twice as much, so the total factor is 6. Long-context attention, activation recomputation, communication and hardware utilization are not included. So 6ND6ND6ND compares the arithmetic budget within a family of models, but it does not convert into GPU hours, and MoE models or very long contexts cannot simply use the total parameter count.

Part 1: Kaplan vs. Chinchilla

Kaplan et al. found that the loss falls approximately as a power law over a wide range, so scale keeps helping, with diminishing returns. Their analysis of compute allocation favored big models and gave N∗∝C0.73N^*\propto C^{0.73}N∗∝C0.73. The experiments mainly used WebText2, counted the parameters without embeddings, mostly used a context of 1024, and reached about 1.5B non-embedding parameters (paper §2, §3, §6).

Chinchilla measured model size and training length again. Besides tracing the lower envelope of the loss along the training curves, it directly compared configurations at fixed FLOPs in the IsoFLOP experiments. Over 400 models from under 70M to over 16B parameters, trained on 5B to over 400B tokens, three estimation methods gave growth exponents for parameters and data of about 0.50/0.500.50/0.500.50/0.50, 0.49/0.510.49/0.510.49/0.51 and 0.46/0.540.46/0.540.46/0.54 (paper §3, Table 2).

The learning-rate schedule hiding in the data

The most practical reason for the disagreement is the learning-rate schedule. A model planned for 100B tokens may still have a high learning rate when it is checkpointed at 20B, while another model planned for 20B from the start has already finished annealing at that point. So both models have processed the same tokens, but with very different optimization opportunities.

If the checkpoint at 20B is used as “the loss achievable with 20B tokens”, the effect of the data volume is mixed with an unfinished annealing. Hoffmann et al. analyze this schedule mismatch explicitly. What is actually measured is closer to

L(N,D;h,D,T),L(N,D;h,\mathcal D,\mathcal T),L(N,D;h,D,T),

where hhh includes the training method and the hyperparameters, D\mathcal DD is the data distribution, and T\mathcal TT covers the tokenizer and the evaluation. Writing L(N,D)L(N,D)L(N,D) therefore means taking a 2D slice with these quantities fixed or tuned.

The difference grows with scale. If the budget is scaled by 1000× from the same starting point, the exponent 0.73 gives about 154.9 times more parameters, while the exponent 0.49 gives only about 29.5 times more. So curves that look similar over a small range lead to configurations that differ by several times after extrapolation.

Llama 3 applies exactly this lesson by calibrating the law on its own experiments. The authors ran IsoFLOP sweeps on models from 40M to 16B with budgets from 6×10186\times10^{18}6×1018 to 102210^{22}1022 FLOPs. Extrapolated to 3.8×10253.8\times10^{25}3.8×1025, the sweep recommended about 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens, and the authors chose 405B. They also built a separate mapping from the negative log-likelihood of the correct answer to the task accuracy in order to predict downstream results (Llama 3 §3.2.1, Figures 2–4).

Three steps need to be kept apart:

  • Experiments answer the question “how does the loss change under these conditions?”
  • Optimization answers the question “where on the surface should the budget go?”
  • A final check asks “what does this loss mean for task performance?”

If these steps are compressed into “bigger is better”, the useful part is lost.

Solving for the optimum under a fixed budget

A parametric fit gives a surface that can be differentiated:

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ.L(N,D)=E+\frac{A}{N^\alpha}+\frac{B}{D^\beta}.L(N,D)=E+NαA​+DβB​.

A,B,E,α,βA,B,E,\alpha,\betaA,B,E,α,β are fitted from training runs. EEE is the asymptote, and the other two terms are the loss that remains because of a finite model and finite training. This decomposition contains an additivity assumption: too few parameters and too little data each have their own cost, and the two costs add up (Chinchilla §3.3, Appendix D.2). A later section returns to this assumption.

At a fixed budget CCC, D=C/(6N)D=C/(6N)D=C/(6N), so doubling the parameters halves the tokens. Substituting gives

LC(N)=E+AN−α+B(6NC)β.L_C(N)=E+AN^{-\alpha} +B\left(\frac{6N}{C}\right)^\beta.LC​(N)=E+AN−α+B(C6N​)β.

The first term falls as NNN grows, and the second term rises. The minimum satisfies

αAN−α=βBD−β.\alpha AN^{-\alpha} =\beta BD^{-\beta}.αAN−α=βBD−β.

Both sides are the marginal loss from moving the same fraction of the budget, so α\alphaα and β\betaβ cannot be dropped, and the two loss terms cannot simply be set equal. Solving gives

N∗(C)=(αAβB)1/(α+β)(C6)β/(α+β),N^*(C)= \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)} \left(\frac C6\right)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)},N∗(C)=(βBαA​)1/(α+β)(6C​)β/(α+β), D∗(C)=(αAβB)−1/(α+β)(C6)α/(α+β).D^*(C)= \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{-1/(\alpha+\beta)} \left(\frac C6\right)^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)}.D∗(C)=(βBαA​)−1/(α+β)(6C​)α/(α+β).

The ratio is hidden in these two expressions. Only when α=β\alpha=\betaα=β do parameters and data both grow as C\sqrt CC​, so that D/ND/ND/N stays constant. In general,

D∗N∗∝C(α−β)/(α+β).\frac{D^*}{N^*}\propto C^{(\alpha-\beta)/(\alpha+\beta)}.N∗D∗​∝C(α−β)/(α+β).

“20 tokens per parameter” is a convenient approximation within one set of experiments. The equation does not force this ratio to be constant, and the ratio is not meant as the point at which training should stop.

Part 2: when the data runs out

So far, every extra unit of DDD was assumed to bring a corresponding value. If the unique corpus has only UUU tokens, training for KKK epochs processes D=KUD=KUD=KU tokens, but the compute of the second epoch is not worth as much as another fresh corpus.

The GPU does not perform fewer matrix multiplications on samples that it has seen before, so the cost is still about 6NKU6NKU6NKU. What changes is how much loss improvement this extra compute brings. Muennighoff et al. describe the decay with an effective quantity of data:

RD=K−1,Deff=U+URD∗(1−e−RD/RD∗).R_D=K-1,\qquad D_{\mathrm{eff}} =U+UR_D^*\left(1-e^{-R_D/R_D^*}\right).RD​=K−1,Deff​=U+URD∗​(1−e−RD​/RD∗​).

The first epoch counts fully as UUU, while later repetitions have a decreasing marginal value, with a decay constant RD∗R_D^*RD∗​ estimated from experiments. The paper also constructs an NeffN_{\mathrm{eff}}Neff​ for model sizes beyond what the data ratio needs, and it inserts both quantities into the loss (Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models §3).

Differentiating with respect to the actually processed tokens DDD gives:

∂Deff∂D=e−(K−1)/RD∗.\frac{\partial D_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial D} =e^{-(K-1)/R_D^*}.∂D∂Deff​​=e−(K−1)/RD∗​.

This derivative is easier to reason about than any number for a “maximum of epochs”. It states how many effective tokens one more processed token is worth in the fitted function at the current point. At K=1+RD∗K=1+R_D^*K=1+RD∗​, the derivative is still e−1e^{-1}e−1, so nothing suddenly fails, and the marginal value halves at 1+RD∗log⁡21+R_D^*\log21+RD∗​log2.

A finite RD∗R_D^*RD∗​ makes the effective data approach U(1+RD∗)U(1+R_D^*)U(1+RD∗​), but this limit is not a physical cap on the information in a corpus, because a different training method uses the same data differently. The extended-training experiment of PaLM is a useful reminder. When a 62B model was extended from about 795B to 1.325T tokens, refreshing the corpus worked better than repeating a sub-corpus, but the refresh also changed the mixture proportions and added arXiv and math web pages. The mean of nine English NLP metrics rose from 64.8 to 66.5, so the gain reflects both changes, not the repetition rate alone (PaLM Appendix F, Tables 31–33).

Inside the public fitting code

The pinned datablations notebook makes this concrete. The plain surface parameterizes the positive coefficients as A=ea,B=eb,E=eeA=e^a,B=e^b,E=e^eA=ea,B=eb,E=ee (the last eee is a log parameter) and then computes:

pre_lse = torch.stack([a - alpha*torch.log(inp[:, 0]), b - beta*torch.log(inp[:, 1]), e.expand((inp.shape[0]))])
post_lse = torch.logsumexp(pre_lse, dim=0)
huber_loss = torch.nn.functional.huber_loss(post_lse, torch.log(inp[:, 2]), delta=1e-3, reduction='none')

The prediction is

log⁡L^=LSE⁡(a−αlog⁡N, b−βlog⁡D, e).\log\widehat L= \operatorname{LSE} (a-\alpha\log N,\ b-\beta\log D,\ e).logL=LSE(a−αlogN, b−βlogD, e).

This form keeps the coefficients positive and computes the residuals on the log loss. Near the observation, log⁡L^−log⁡L\log\widehat L-\log LlogL−logL approximates the relative error (L^−L)/L(\widehat L-L)/L(L−L)/L, and the Huber loss makes large residuals grow roughly linearly, so a few outliers cannot dominate the fit.

The version for repeated data replaces the second term with the effective quantity:

b - beta * torch.log(inp[:, 1] + (inp[:, 1] * ep_star * (1 - torch.exp(-1*inp[:, 2]/ep_star))))

The two columns are UUU and the extra repetitions RDR_DRD​, and ep_star is RD∗R_D^*RD∗​; the parameter term receives the same treatment. With fixed=True, the gradients of the five base parameters are set to zero, and only the two decay constants are fitted. This is a staged fit: the value of repetition is added on top of a previously fitted C4 surface, instead of a joint estimate of all seven parameters.

Part 3: when parameters and data interact

Every correction so far kept the additive structure, and this structure has a directly testable consequence. For two unique data budgets U1<U2U_1<U_2U1​<U2​,

L(N,U1)−L(N,U2)=B(U1−β−U2−β),L(N,U_1)-L(N,U_2) =B(U_1^{-\beta}-U_2^{-\beta}),L(N,U1​)−L(N,U2​)=B(U1−β​−U2−β​),

and the right side does not contain NNN. So as the model grows, the loss gap between two data budgets should stay constant.

If the real gap changes systematically with the model size, re-estimating the five coefficients cannot fix the model, and the structure of the surface must change. Data-Constrained Language Model Pretraining: Improved Regularization and Scaling Laws studies exactly this question. Across five sizes from about 72M to 1.4B parameters, each with 100M, 200M, 300M and 400M unique tokens, the authors built 20 DCLM baseline points. They trained on eight H100 GPUs with 80 GB at a context of 2048, with tuned epochs and regularization (paper §2, §4, Appendices A–B).

Note that the question has changed: it is now how low the loss can go with fixed unique data and adequate training, not the question about fixed FLOPs from before. The SoftQ form of the authors is

L(N,U)=E+(ANρ+BUρ/(1+α))α/ρ.L(N,U)=E+ \left( \frac A{N^\rho} +\frac B{U^{\rho/(1+\alpha)}} \right)^{\alpha/\rho}.L(N,U)=E+(NρA​+Uρ/(1+α)B​)α/ρ.

Its α,ρ\alpha,\rhoα,ρ belong to the new model and cannot be mixed with the values of Chinchilla. Call the two terms in the brackets x,yx,yx,y and the outer exponent qqq. Then

∂(x+y)q∂y=q(x+y)q−1.\frac{\partial(x+y)^q}{\partial y}=q(x+y)^{q-1}.∂y∂(x+y)q​=q(x+y)q−1.

As long as q≠1q\ne1q=1, the parameter term xxx changes the marginal effect of the data term. This coupling is exactly what the additive form lacks.

Five held-out points at neighboring sizes support this local improvement of the structure, but an extrapolation from 1.4B to hundreds of billions of parameters is not validated. In addition, a surface with tuned epochs cannot be inserted back into 6ND=C6ND=C6ND=C, because the training length was already optimized when the surface was built.

This work also uses masked-input regularization: it adds an auxiliary loss on masked inputs to the clean next-token loss. This regularization improves the use of unique data but costs extra compute, so better data efficiency comes with a trade-off: getting more out of the same corpus is not the same as getting more out of the same FLOPs.

Part 4: when the model has to be served

Model selection has another real-world condition: how many requests the model will answer after training.

The 405B model of Llama 3 sits near the training-compute optimum estimated by the authors, while its smaller models were deliberately trained for longer. Adding more data to a small model may already be worse than spending the same training compute on a bigger model; however, once the small model reaches the required quality, every future request costs a little less (Llama 3 §1, §3.2.1).

Sardana et al. add the inference cost to the selection objective (Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal §2). To compare configurations of equal quality, fix the target loss at ℓ\ellℓ and let III be the inference tokens over the lifetime of the model:

min⁡N,D 6ND+2NIs.t.L(N,D)=ℓ.\min_{N,D}\ 6ND+2NI \quad\text{s.t.}\quad L(N,D)=\ell.N,Dmin​ 6ND+2NIs.t.L(N,D)=ℓ.

The first term is paid once, while the second term accumulates with use. On the additive loss surface, the data needed to reach the target is

D(N)=(Bℓ−E−AN−α)1/β,N>(Aℓ−E)1/α.D(N)= \left(\frac B{\ell-E-AN^{-\alpha}}\right)^{1/\beta}, \qquad N>\left(\frac A{\ell-E}\right)^{1/\alpha}.D(N)=(ℓ−E−AN−αB​)1/β,N>(ℓ−EA​)1/α.

This lower bound matters: in this function, a model that is small enough cannot reach the target even with infinite training. Near the bound, D(N)D(N)D(N) diverges, so a smaller model is not always cheaper.

Differentiating the cost gives:

J′(N)=6(D+ND′)+2I,J'(N)=6\bigl(D+ND'\bigr)+2I,J′(N)=6(D+ND′)+2I, D′(N)=−D αAN−α−1β(ℓ−E−AN−α).D'(N)= -\frac{D\,\alpha AN^{-\alpha-1}} {\beta(\ell-E-AN^{-\alpha})}.D′(N)=−β(ℓ−E−AN−α)DαAN−α−1​.

With training cost only, the zero of the derivative is the point where the first term vanishes. When a positive III is added, the derivative at this point becomes positive, so shrinking the model slightly lowers the total cost, while more training tokens make up for the loss. The shift toward smaller models therefore comes from the constraint of equal quality, not from an assumption that small models are better.

At the end with high demand, the recommendation is extremely long training, and this regime is exactly where the extrapolation of the loss fails first. Sardana et al. trained 47 MPT models from 150M to 6B parameters on non-repeated web and code data. The extreme ratio of 10,000 tokens per parameter was tried only at 150M, without full coverage at larger sizes. The authors also found that curves fitted at conventional ratios overestimate the benefit of extremely long training (same paper §3–5).

So this figure explains why training small models for longer is worth it, but it does not guarantee that a specific product can skip validation in the regime of very long training. If the constraint of unique data from Part 2 also applies, the assumption that “fresh data is available” must change again.

The real cost also depends on the actual serving. Prefill processes the inputs at once, while decode generates the output token by token, and equal FLOPs can run at different utilization because of batching, the KV cache and bandwidth limits. An objective closer to deployment is

Cost=ctrain(N,D)+Q E[cprefill(N,P)+cdecode(N,P,T)],\mathrm{Cost} =c_{\mathrm{train}}(N,D) +Q\,\mathbb E[ c_{\mathrm{prefill}}(N,P) +c_{\mathrm{decode}}(N,P,T)],Cost=ctrain​(N,D)+QE[cprefill​(N,P)+cdecode​(N,P,T)],

with QQQ requests and input and output lengths P,TP,TP,T. These cost functions must be measured on the target hardware, with the target parallel configuration and latency requirements. A model that shrinks just enough to fit on one GPU may move to a cheaper price level, so saved FLOPs do not necessarily translate into savings in the same ratio.

Part 5: when inference itself is a choice

The inference volume III is not necessarily a fixed constant either. The same question can be answered once, or with several candidates from which a verifier chooses, or with a revision after the first answer is read. The strategy decides how many tokens are spent, and it also changes the probability of a correct answer.

Call this strategy π\piπ. The objective then becomes

max⁡N,D,πEx[Score(x;N,D,π)]s.t.6ND+Q Ex[c(x;N,π)]≤Ctotal.\max_{N,D,\pi} \mathbb E_x[\mathrm{Score}(x;N,D,\pi)] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad 6ND+Q\,\mathbb E_x[c(x;N,\pi)] \le C_{\mathrm{total}}.N,D,πmax​Ex​[Score(x;N,D,π)]s.t.6ND+QEx​[c(x;N,π)]≤Ctotal​.

This objective does not produce a new universal recipe, but it adds back the missing decision variable. The pretraining cross-entropy alone cannot decide whether a correct answer is among the candidates, and even less whether a verifier can select it.

Snell et al. used the MATH split with 12k training and 500 test problems and PaLM 2-S* models trained for revision or verification to compare sequential revision with candidate search, and they found that the right allocation of the budget differs by difficulty. (Estimating the difficulty in this way, with 2048 samples per question, has its own cost in addition to the main analysis.) (paper §3–4).

On easy questions near saturation, ten times more candidates may only waste compute, while on very hard questions where correct solutions are almost never sampled, an evaluator cannot choose a candidate that does not exist. In practice, allocating the budget requires estimating which requests still benefit from more compute, using only information that is available at serving time. The post on self-correction, selection and process supervision discusses these differences in detail.

Conclusion

The opening question was whether to train 7B on 140B tokens or 70B on 14B tokens. If the only question is the validation loss at fixed training FLOPs, solving along the Chinchilla surface gives the answer. Each new condition, however, moves this answer:

  • Finite data reduces the value of repeated tokens, and the fitted curve of effective data cannot express overfitting.
  • Coupling of parameters and data breaks the prediction of the additive surface that data gaps do not depend on the model size.
  • Deployment pushes choices of equal quality toward smaller models trained for longer, until the extrapolation is no longer reliable.
  • Inference-time strategy turns part of the compute budget into a separate choice.

A single fixed ratio of tokens per parameter leaves out exactly what scaling laws do best: helping to make these decisions.

A scaling law is not a recipe; it is a way to compute the recipe for the actual conditions.
