Evaluating Navigation Corrections on Held-Out Data with Shared Errors

Holding out later observations is the right idea, but in navigation all segments share the same inertial, map and device errors. This post shows how to freeze a candidate correction and still score it correctly.

Suppose a stretch of WiFi observations supports a correction to a trajectory. A few seconds later, a new batch of observations arrives. If the candidate correction is refitted with the new batch included, its score goes up. This looks good, but because the new batch has now helped choose the model, the new batch can no longer show whether the model was right.

Machine learning has a standard answer to this problem: hold data out. The candidate is built on segments A and B and then frozen, and segment C is used only to score a prediction. This idea is correct, and it is the subject of this post.

Navigation, however, has a complication that a random train/test split does not have. A, B and C can share the same inertial error, the same fingerprint map and the same RSS bias of the device. So although the three segments do not overlap in time, they are still not independent. Separation in time is not statistical independence.

The post starts with a small example that shows why. It then builds the protocol step by step: checking that A and B agree, deciding exactly what is frozen, scoring C by integrating over the state, and keeping the score records accurate. The last section asks what this score would need in order to become a real sequential test. The running example is a frozen candidate from a flat-floor walking log (T31).

Fundamentals: time separation isn’t independence

The core problem fits in two equations. Two observations share a common bias bbb:

z1=x1+b+ϵ1,z2=x2+b+ϵ2.z_1=x_1+b+\epsilon_1,\qquad z_2=x_2+b+\epsilon_2.z1​=x1​+b+ϵ1​,z2​=x2​+b+ϵ2​.

Even if the noises ϵ1\epsilon_1ϵ1​ and ϵ2\epsilon_2ϵ2​ are independent, z1z_1z1​ and z2z_2z2​ share uncertainty as long as bbb is unknown. Taking the second observation ten seconds later does not remove bbb. Errors in the fingerprint map, the RSS bias of the device, the initial attitude and overlapping inertial inputs all behave like bbb.

In the linear-Gaussian case the joint distribution is

[z1z2]∼N ⁣([μ1μ2],[S11S12S21S22]),\begin{bmatrix}z_1\\z_2\end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\!\left( \begin{bmatrix}\mu_1\\\mu_2\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix} \right),[z1​z2​​]∼N([μ1​μ2​​],[S11​S21​​S12​S22​​]),

and once z1z_1z1​ is known, the correct prediction for z2z_2z2​ is the conditional distribution

μ2∣1=μ2+S21S11−1(z1−μ1),S2∣1=S22−S21S11−1S12.\mu_{2\mid1}=\mu_2+S_{21}S_{11}^{-1}(z_1-\mu_1),\qquad S_{2\mid1}=S_{22}-S_{21}S_{11}^{-1}S_{12}.μ2∣1​=μ2​+S21​S11−1​(z1​−μ1​),S2∣1​=S22​−S21​S11−1​S12​.

In code, this should be computed by solving the linear system, not by forming the inverse. Note that conditioning moves the mean, not only the variance. For this reason, correlation is not a uniform “discount factor”: a new observation that moves along the shared bias may be less surprising than it looks, while a new observation that breaks the shared pattern may be more surprising.

A numerical example makes this concrete. Let the variance of the common bias be 4 and both noise variances be 1, with the known x1,x2x_1,x_2x1​,x2​ removed:

S=[5445].S=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{bmatrix}.S=[54​45​].

Suppose the first residual is 2. Then the prediction for the second residual has mean 4/5×2=1.64/5\times2=1.64/5×2=1.6 and variance 5−16/5=1.85-16/5=1.85−16/5=1.8. If the first observation were ignored, the prediction would have mean 0 and variance 5.

This result is neither “halve the weight of the second observation” nor “throw the second observation away”. The first observation helped identify the shared bias, so the second observation should be tested around the updated prediction. The rest of the post applies this one idea carefully.

The protocol: build on A and B, freeze, predict C

Figure 1: Freezing fixes both the construction result and the scoring rule. Common uncertainty still flows across every stage. C is only scored; it never flows back to pick a candidate.

A few terms are used throughout. KEEP is the existing explanation. A candidate correction is described by a parameter θ\thetaθ. This parameter can be a translation, a finite rotation or a change to a stretch of state constraints. A, B and C are segments assigned by the protocol, not places on a map. Other experiments also have snapshots called A–D, but those snapshots are unrelated.

Step 1: do A and B agree?

Construction starts by estimating θ^A\hat\theta_Aθ^A​ and θ^B\hat\theta_Bθ^B​ separately and checking that the two estimates are compatible. The difference d=θ^A−θ^Bd=\hat\theta_A-\hat\theta_Bd=θ^A​−θ^B​ has covariance

Cov⁡(d)=PA+PB−PAB−PABT.\operatorname{Cov}(d)=P_A+P_B-P_{AB}-P_{AB}^{T}.Cov(d)=PA​+PB​−PAB​−PABT​.

Dropping the cross term silently assumes that it is zero, and the small example above showed that this assumption is wrong. Two failure modes follow:

  • A shared error pulls both segments toward the same wrong answer. The two segments then agree very well, but this agreement shows only that they are compatible, not that they are right.
  • Forcing independence computes the uncertainty of ddd incorrectly. Depending on the correlation, the check then becomes too loose or too strict.

Step 2: build the joint candidate without double counting

If A and B pass the check, they are combined into one joint candidate that uses all the construction information. The danger here is double counting. Two posteriors that already contain the same initial prior cannot be fused as if they were two independent measurements. The joint candidate must either go back to the original factors and the common source, or use an equivalent representation in which the conditioning set and the cross-covariances are explicit. “Average the two posteriors” skips the one question that matters.

Step 3: freeze

Freezing covers more than the numbers of the candidate. At minimum, the following items are frozen:

  • the candidate parameters and their construction sources;
  • the approximation of the state posterior;
  • the noise model, the mode weights and the NULL explanation;
  • the grouping of correlated observations and the observation bindings;
  • the numerical integration rule and the candidate selection policy;
  • the risk budget, if there is one.

In this case, the three original WiFi modes and their masses are kept unchanged instead of being averaged into one center. The noise is a Student-t distribution with ν=4\nu=4ν=4 that uses the provider’s QQQ as the scale matrix, so the covariance of a single mode is 2Q2Q2Q. The NULL branch keeps its frozen weight and density and is not recalibrated against later ground truth.

Freezing does not make any of these choices correct. What freezing provides is that C tests the same claim that was written down before C arrived, instead of a claim whose meaning changed with the results. If the noise model is wrong, freezing makes the error visible instead of hiding it.

The second difficulty concerns time. Each C observation in the flat-floor walk has four different timestamps:

Sequential prediction depends on what information was available, so these four times cannot be merged into one. A number computed on the state at 286 s was not necessarily available at 286 s. If a background task finished later, an online system could only use the result after that time. The experiment restores the recorded requests and source boundaries but not the completion times of the original asynchronous Radio tasks, so it analyzes the information sources on fixed inputs rather than reproducing the online response.

Estimates also change as new information arrives. At its own cutoff, the predicted mean of C1 is about (−34.346,27.182)(-34.346,27.182)(−34.346,27.182) m. Past states can legitimately move in this way. A score that has already been recorded, however, must not move with them, and a later section explains why.

Scoring C: integrate over the state, not the mean

The frozen likelihood of the iii-th observation is a mixture of Student-t modes plus a NULL background:

gi(xi)=(1−πi)∑kwik t4(zik−xi;Qi)+πici,g_i(x_i)=(1-\pi_i)\sum_k w_{ik}\, t_4(z_{ik}-x_i;Q_i)+\pi_i c_i,gi​(xi​)=(1−πi​)k∑​wik​t4​(zik​−xi​;Qi​)+πi​ci​,

with mode masses wikw_{ik}wik​, NULL weight πi\pi_iπi​ and background density cic_ici​. The prediction score integrates over the state uncertainty instead of inserting the mean of the trajectory:

q(C1,C2)=∫g1(x1)g2(x2)N(x;μ,P) dx.q(C_1,C_2)=\int g_1(x_1)g_2(x_2) \mathcal N(x;\mu,P)\,dx.q(C1​,C2​)=∫g1​(x1​)g2​(x2​)N(x;μ,P)dx.

The product g1(x1)g2(x2)g_1(x_1)g_2(x_2)g1​(x1​)g2​(x2​) contains an assumption that should be stated: given the states, the two observation mechanisms are independent. Keeping the cross-covariance of the states does not model any correlated error that remains between the two scans. If a shared device bias is not part of the state, the product can still be wrong. This assumption belongs to the frozen model, and no amount of integration accuracy can prove it.

A trick for the integral

Integrating a Student-t mixture against a 4D Gaussian sounds expensive. The trick is the Gaussian scale-mixture form of the Student-t distribution. For a 2D observation with ν=4\nu=4ν=4:

λ∼Gamma⁡(2,2),e∣λ∼N(0,Q/λ),\lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(2,2),\qquad e\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal N(0,Q/\lambda),λ∼Gamma(2,2),e∣λ∼N(0,Q/λ),

where the second Gamma parameter is a rate. For each fixed λi\lambda_iλi​, the state integral is just a Gaussian convolution: add Qi/λiQ_i/\lambda_iQi​/λi​ block by block to the joint state covariance PPP, and evaluate a multivariate Gaussian density at the observed mode positions. NULL branches have a constant density, so they only need the remaining state dimensions to be marginalized. The computation therefore enumerates the mode combinations and performs a 1D quadrature over each λ\lambdaλ, instead of building a grid over the 4D position space.

The historical implementation used adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature. The independent recomputation uses generalized Gauss–Laguerre quadrature instead: with u=2λu=2\lambdau=2λ the weight is ue−uu e^{-u}ue−u, so α=1\alpha=1α=1. The recomputation ran with 16, 32, 64 and 128 nodes and never called the historical scorer:

# For each non-NULL mode combination; Q is the Student-t scale matrix
nodes, weights = roots_genlaguerre(order, alpha=1)
precision = nodes / 2
covariance = state_joint_covariance + block_diag(Q1 / lam1, Q2 / lam2)
value += mixture_weight * quadrature_weight * gaussian_density(
    observed_mode_positions, state_mean, covariance
)

The ledger: why the first score stays on the books

This section returns to the estimates that change over time. Mark each information cutoff with a subscript. The score that this case accumulates is

S2=log⁡qt1(C1)+log⁡qt2(C1,C2)qt2(C1).S_2=\log q_{t_1}(C_1) +\log\frac{q_{t_2}(C_1,C_2)}{q_{t_2}(C_1)}.S2​=logqt1​​(C1​)+logqt2​​(C1​)qt2​​(C1​,C2​)​.

The first term was computed and frozen at t1t_1t1​. The second term is the conditional density of C2 under the common prediction at t2t_2t2​. If the first term were replaced with log⁡qt2(C1)\log q_{t_2}(C_1)logqt2​​(C1​), the sum would become the joint log-density at the later cutoff. That sum looks cleaner, but it silently rewrites the first entry in the ledger with information that did not exist when the entry was recorded.

Figure 3: Frozen sequential total vs. later joint density. The gap of about 0.146236 is not floating-point error; the first term was computed under different information. At the later cutoff, conditional C2 scores about 0.540286 higher than C2’s marginal alone.

The value 0.540286 is the small example again: keeping the correlation structure changes how surprising C2 is. The value 0.146236, on the other hand, is the cost of rewriting history. For this reason, “rescore everything against the latest trajectory and add up the results” is tempting but risky, because this method either reuses old observations or treats later information as if it had been known earlier.

One more caution is needed. There is no rule that a more negative score means a worse candidate. Continuous densities carry units, so absolute log-densities from different observation spaces cannot be compared. To compare a candidate with KEEP, both predictive distributions must be built on the same observations, the same base measure and the same available information.

From a score to a test

It is tempting to go one step further and turn this score into a stopping rule. When both hypotheses H1H_1H1​ and H0H_0H0​ define valid one-step conditional distributions, the following product can be formed:

Lt=∏i=1tp1(zi∣Fi−1)p0(zi∣Fi−1).L_t=\prod_{i=1}^{t} \frac{p_1(z_i\mid\mathcal F_{i-1})}{p_0(z_i\mid\mathcal F_{i-1})}.Lt​=i=1∏t​p0​(zi​∣Fi−1​)p1​(zi​∣Fi−1​)​.

If the requirements on support, on the conditional model and on predictable selection all hold, a simple null hypothesis gives E0[Lt∣Ft−1]=Lt−1\mathbb E_0[L_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=L_{t-1}E0​[Lt​∣Ft−1​]=Lt−1​, and the crossing bound of a non-negative (super)martingale then shows when stopping is allowed. Even in this case, if the next message is a deterministic copy of the previous one, it carries no new information, and its likelihood ratio cannot be multiplied in again.

Real navigation rarely meets these conditions. Variances are estimated, candidates are pre-selected, mode masses are empirical and the background distribution is only partly known, so a score that keeps improving does not yet carry these guarantees. With a composite null hypothesis, the property must hold for the entire specified class of null distributions, not only in expectation under one fitted reference model.

📝 Further reading: Sequential testing of conditionally constrained hypotheses studies hypothesis classes defined by finitely many conditional constraints, and products of predictable one-step e-variables. It shows clearly how much the conditional model and predictable selection matter, and why an arbitrary correlated score can’t simply be wrapped into a safe sequential test.1

Conclusion

Held-out segments are still the right idea in navigation, but because the segments share errors, every step must carry the cross-covariance: the compatibility check between A and B, the joint construction, and the prediction for C. Freezing fixes the rules, not the truth. Each observation has several timestamps, and only the availability boundary shows what was known at which time. Scores are integrated over the state uncertainty, and the ledger keeps each entry under the information that was used to compute it.

In practice, this means recording the model version, the information cutoff and the observation sources of each score. Numerical results that did not converge are recorded as “unresolved”. A reused common prior means that the joint model must be fixed. If the candidate was changed after C was examined, the data must be split again into construction data and test data. Each of these rules guards against a different statistical error.

In the end, the whole protocol reduces to one question: before seeing this C, could the prediction of the candidate have been written down? If the frozen object is defined that clearly, C can test it. If it is not, C simply becomes part of another fit.
