Replacing Gaussian Noise with Student-t in Robust WiFi Positioning

If a Student-t with 4 degrees of freedom receives the Q of a Gaussian, its covariance doubles. Scale, central pull and tail all change at once; this post separates which change moved a robust WiFi solution, including the failures.

“Our WiFi positions have outliers, so let’s use a Student-t instead of a Gaussian.” This is one of the most common robustness changes in estimation, and it sounds like a one-line change: keep the covariance QQQ and swap the distribution.

The first result is surprising. If a Student-t with 4 degrees of freedom receives the same QQQ as its scale matrix, the resulting distribution has covariance 2Q2Q2Q, because the covariance of a Student-t is ν/(ν−2)\nu/(\nu-2)ν/(ν−2) times its scale matrix. The intention was to change the tail, but the change also doubled the covariance.

A natural next step is to compare against a Gaussian with covariance 2Q2Q2Q instead. This comparison matches the covariance, but now the center does not match: in the original whitened coordinates, the Gaussian with 2Q pulls with a constant gradient coefficient of 1/21/21/2, while the Student-t starts at 3/23/23/2 and then decays. So the same observations lead to different stationary trajectories.

On a real flat-floor walking log (T31), this difference matters. In snapshot A, the Student-t reduces the position error dramatically, while in snapshot C it is worse than both Gaussians. This post separates the causes. It first builds up the fundamentals: scale versus covariance, influence functions and probability regions. It then examines the flat-floor snapshots and one observation whose weight drops to 0.14. After that, it runs a control with an equal budget that changes the conclusion for B and C, checks that the solver optimizes the intended objective, and finally explains why normalization constants can reverse the ranking of models.

Fundamentals: scale matrix vs. covariance

The whole post works in whitened coordinates. With the residual e=pEN+a−ze=p_{EN}+a-ze=pEN​+a−z and Q=LL⊤Q=LL^\topQ=LL⊤:

r=L−1e,s=r⊤r=e⊤Q−1e.r=L^{-1}e,\qquad s=r^\top r=e^\top Q^{-1}e.r=L−1e,s=r⊤r=e⊤Q−1e.

eee is in meters and QQQ in m², while rrr and sss have no units. When likelihoods are compared, it matters to stay in the coordinates of the original QQQ, so that “two standard deviations” means the same thing on every plot.

The negative log-density of the Gaussian with Q is

ℓG(e;Q)=log⁡(2π)+12log⁡∣Q∣+12s,\ell_G(e;Q)=\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|Q|+\frac12s,ℓG​(e;Q)=log(2π)+21​log∣Q∣+21​s,

where QQQ is the covariance. The 2D Student-t is

pt(e;ν,S)=Γ((ν+2)/2)Γ(ν/2) νπ ∣S∣1/2(1+e⊤S−1eν)−(ν+2)/2,p_t(e;\nu,S)= \frac{\Gamma((\nu+2)/2)}{\Gamma(\nu/2)\,\nu\pi\,|S|^{1/2}} \left(1+\frac{e^\top S^{-1}e}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+2)/2},pt​(e;ν,S)=Γ(ν/2)νπ∣S∣1/2Γ((ν+2)/2)​(1+νe⊤S−1e​)−(ν+2)/2,

where SSS is a scale matrix, not a covariance. For ν>2\nu>2ν>2,

Cov⁡(e)=νν−2S.\operatorname{Cov}(e)=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}S.Cov(e)=ν−2ν​S.

At ν=4\nu=4ν=4 and S=QS=QS=Q, the covariance is therefore 2Q2Q2Q. The literature on Student-t filtering makes this point repeatedly, and Roth et al. also show that matching only the covariance makes the center of the substitute distribution too peaked (paper, §2.1–2.2, §4.3).

With S=cQS=cQS=cQ, the 2D Gamma factors cancel, and a convenient unified form remains:

ℓt(e;ν,cQ)=log⁡(2π)+12log⁡∣Q∣+log⁡c+ν+22log⁡(1+sνc).\ell_t(e;\nu,cQ) =\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|Q|+\log c +\frac{\nu+2}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{s}{\nu c}\right).ℓt​(e;ν,cQ)=log(2π)+21​log∣Q∣+logc+2ν+2​log(1+νcs​).

With this form, it is possible to check what each candidate comparison actually matches. At ν=4\nu=4ν=4:

Central curvature is the second derivative of the negative log-density at r=0r=0r=0. The lesson of the table is that no single comparison matches everything: if the covariance is fixed, the central curvature, the 95% region and the far tail still differ. So the historical control with 2Q answers a narrower question than it seems to answer, namely whether enlarging the covariance alone can explain the gain.

Fundamentals: who pulls how hard

The gradient shows how strongly each observation pulls on the state. Without the constants,

∇rℓt=w(s)r,w(s)=ν+2νc+s.\nabla_r\ell_t=w(s)r, \qquad w(s)=\frac{\nu+2}{\nu c+s}.∇r​ℓt​=w(s)r,w(s)=νc+sν+2​.

The Gaussian with Q has w=1w=1w=1, and the Gaussian with 2Q has w=1/2w=1/2w=1/2. The historical Student-t has

w(s)=64+s.w(s)=\frac6{4+s}.w(s)=4+s6​.

This function gives w=1.5w=1.5w=1.5 at s=0s=0s=0, equals the value 1 of the Gaussian with Q at s=2s=2s=2, and drops to the value 0.5 of the Gaussian with 2Q only at s=8s=8s=8. So the Student-t pulls harder than either Gaussian on small residuals and only lets go on large residuals. “Robust” does not mean “softer everywhere”.

It is also important to state what www is not. It is not the probability that an observation is trustworthy: it can exceed 1, and it depends on the current state. If the initial trajectory is far from the truth, a genuinely good observation appears as a large residual and receives a low weight. Conversely, a group of observations that agree with each other but all sit on the wrong position island keeps large weights together. The Student-t does not decide which mode is real.

The same weight also follows directly from the view of the Student-t as a Gaussian scale mixture:

λ∼Gamma⁡(ν/2,ν/2),e∣λ∼N(0,cQ/λ),\lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2), \qquad e\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal N(0,cQ/\lambda),λ∼Gamma(ν/2,ν/2),e∣λ∼N(0,cQ/λ),

where the second Gamma parameter is a rate. Given the residual, this gives

λ∣e∼Gamma⁡(ν+22,ν+s/c2),E[λ∣e]c=ν+2νc+s.\lambda\mid e\sim \operatorname{Gamma}\left(\frac{\nu+2}{2},\frac{\nu+s/c}{2}\right), \qquad \frac{\mathbb E[\lambda\mid e]}c=\frac{\nu+2}{\nu c+s}.λ∣e∼Gamma(2ν+2​,2ν+s/c​),cE[λ∣e]​=νc+sν+2​.

This is the origin of the phrase “the precision adapts to the residual”. It does not mean that every solver must estimate λ\lambdaλ: this project computes the exact marginal negative log-likelihood directly and does not produce a calibrated fault probability.

The setup: four overlapping snapshots of one walk

After the theory, the post turns to the data. A, B, C and D are four overlapping snapshots from the same log. Each snapshot was solved offline from the same Motion history, starting at 68.5 s, but with its own batch of conditional Radio position hypotheses:

These letters are only labels for the snapshots, unrelated to the A/B/C stages of the prediction experiments. The Radio positions come from conditional modes that were already selected, not from a full integration over multiple modes and NULL. Each solution has two free EN translation parameters, and the objective is

F(X,a)=Fprior(X)+FMotion(X)+Fpressure(X)+∑iℓi(pi,EN+a−zi),F(X,a)=F_{\mathrm{prior}}(X) +F_{\mathrm{Motion}}(X) +F_{\mathrm{pressure}}(X) +\sum_i \ell_i\bigl(p_{i,EN}+a-z_i\bigr),F(X,a)=Fprior​(X)+FMotion​(X)+Fpressure​(X)+i∑​ℓi​(pi,EN​+a−zi​),

where XXX holds position, velocity and attitude and a∈R2a\in\mathbb R^2a∈R2 is the free translation. Only the last term changes between the models. Everything else stays fixed: the observations and their Q, the time nodes of the states, the nonlinear Motion, the old startup prior, the parameterization of the free translation, and the pressure per second with its correlation handling. No reference points enter the optimization, and no height ranges are used to choose solutions.

One limitation applies throughout. The historical Motion uses the representation with independent relative factors, and the post on common covariance shows that this assumption can badly distort velocity. A robust Radio loss cannot fix this problem.

Case A: one observation lets go

The first question is how the Student-t weighted the six radio observations of A. The saved radio_likelihood contains each residual and Q, so the following values can be computed directly

si=ei⊤Qi−1ei,wi=64+si,s_i=e_i^\top Q_i^{-1}e_i, \qquad w_i=\frac6{4+s_i},si​=ei⊤​Qi−1​ei​,wi​=4+si​6​,

and checked against the record.

One observation, at 266 s, stands out:

Saved solutionGeometric residual of this observationOriginal-Q squared Mahalanobis distance sGradient coefficient w
Gaussian 2Q4.406 m18.1136680.5
Student-t6.247 m38.0421270.142714

This observation shows the mechanism in miniature. The Student-t did not fit this observation better; it left a larger residual, 6.247 m instead of 4.406 m, while it reduced the pull of the observation to 0.14. The other five observations in A received weights of about 1.009–1.439, well above the constant 0.5 of 2Q. So the trajectory could follow the other observations and the Motion chain more closely, without paying the full price for one conflicting term.

This result does not mean that the observation at 266 s is wrong. The conflict could come from Motion bias, a wrong position mode, timing or unmodeled correlation. The weight only knows the residual under the current joint model, and it does not know which part of the model caused the conflict.

D provides a useful contrast. Its most strongly down-weighted observation, at 390 s, has s≈8.324s\approx8.324s≈8.324 and w≈0.487w\approx0.487w≈0.487, about the same weight as the Gaussian with 2Q. The smaller residuals have different weights, but the final reference errors differ by only about 1.6 cm. “Heavy tails are more robust” is therefore too coarse a summary. What actually predicts the effect is whether a strongly conflicting observation exists, so that the weights can move away from it.

Making the solver optimize the right thing

So far, the post has assumed that the solver minimizes the Student-t objective. This assumption deserves a check, because the standard way of giving a robust loss to a least-squares solver has a classic pitfall.

The historical implementation uses a radial residual transform:

ρ(s)=(ν+2)log⁡(1+s/ν),g(r)=a(s)r,a(s)=ρ(s)s,\rho(s)=(\nu+2)\log(1+s/\nu), \qquad g(r)=a(s)r, \qquad a(s)=\sqrt{\frac{\rho(s)}s},ρ(s)=(ν+2)log(1+s/ν),g(r)=a(s)r,a(s)=sρ(s)​​,

so that 12g⊤g=12ρ(s)\tfrac12g^\top g=\tfrac12\rho(s)21​g⊤g=21​ρ(s), exactly the Student-t negative log-likelihood without constants. As s→0s\to0s→0, a(0)=(ν+2)/νa(0)=\sqrt{(\nu+2)/\nu}a(0)=(ν+2)/ν​, and the code switches to a series expansion near zero to avoid cancellation.

The pitfall is that g = weight * r looks like weighted least squares, although weight depends on rrr. The full Jacobian is

Jg=aI+2a′(s)rr⊤,2a′(s)=ρ′(s)s−ρ(s)a(s)s2,J_g=aI+2a'(s)rr^\top, \qquad 2a'(s)=\frac{\rho'(s)s-\rho(s)}{a(s)s^2},Jg​=aI+2a′(s)rr⊤,2a′(s)=a(s)s2ρ′(s)s−ρ(s)​,

and only with the second term does the gradient come out correctly:

Jg⊤g=ρ′(s)r=ν+2ν+sr.J_g^\top g=\rho'(s)r=\frac{\nu+2}{\nu+s}r.Jg⊤​g=ρ′(s)r=ν+sν+2​r.

Three approaches are often all called “weighted least squares”, but they compute different things:

ApproachWhat it actually computes
Radial transform g, with full JgJ_gJg​Exact Student-t objective and exact first-order gradient, followed by a Gauss–Newton approximation
IRLS with w(s)=ρ′(s)w(s)=\rho'(s)w(s)=ρ′(s), w fixed each roundAn iterative quadratic surrogate whose gradient matches at the current point; weights must be updated and descent checked against the original objective
g=a(s)rg=a(s)rg=a(s)r but dropping the derivative of aNeither of the above; usually doesn’t even match the original objective’s first-order gradient

The historical code implements the first approach correctly. In assemble, it replaces only the Radio residual rows, two per observation, and multiplies the original Jacobian from the left by the full 2D derivative, while the Motion and pressure rows stay unchanged.

To confirm this, we extracted this pure 2D function from the frozen source and tested it at 3 degrees of freedom and 12 residual sizes, including zero, both sides of the series cutoff and large residuals:

Exact Hessian vs. Gauss–Newton

A correct gradient still does not make the Gauss–Newton matrix equal to the true Hessian. In whitened coordinates for ν=4,S=Q\nu=4,S=Qν=4,S=Q,

Hr=64+sI−12(4+s)2rr⊤.H_r=\frac6{4+s}I-\frac{12}{(4+s)^2}rr^\top.Hr​=4+s6​I−(4+s)212​rr⊤.

Perpendicular to the residual, the eigenvalue is 6/(4+s)6/(4+s)6/(4+s); along the residual, it is

λ∥=6(4−s)(4+s)2.\lambda_{\parallel}=\frac{6(4-s)}{(4+s)^2}.λ∥​=(4+s)26(4−s)​.

This eigenvalue becomes negative once s>4s>4s>4, that is, once the whitened radius exceeds 2: the influence function is decreasing there, so the loss is locally concave in the radial direction. Gauss–Newton cannot see this property, because Jg⊤JgJ_g^\top J_gJg⊤​Jg​ is always positive semidefinite, with the radial eigenvalue

λ∥GN=(ρ′(s)a(s))2.\lambda_{\parallel}^{GN}=\left(\frac{\rho'(s)}{a(s)}\right)^2.λ∥GN​=(a(s)ρ′(s)​)2.

Figure 6: Single-factor curvature in a constructed 2D example. This is about one factor, not a claim that the full graph’s Hessian is indefinite.

A concrete point makes the gap clear. At s=25s=25s=25, the true radial curvature is −0.149822, the GN curvature is 0.090035, and the frozen IRLS weight is 0.206897. So one model gives three numbers that describe three different objects.

For the full state, the chain rule adds the second derivative of the residual itself:

Hθ=Jr⊤HrJr+∑j(∇rℓ)j ∇θ2rj.H_\theta =J_r^\top H_rJ_r +\sum_j (\nabla_r\ell)_j\,\nabla_\theta^2r_j.Hθ​=Jr⊤​Hr​Jr​+j∑​(∇r​ℓ)j​∇θ2​rj​.

Other factors can keep the total objective positive definite at a stationary point, so a negative curvature in a single factor does not make the optimization fail. However, if a local matrix is inverted to report uncertainty, the report must state whether the matrix is GN, the true local Laplace curvature or something else, and it must check where this matrix applies. Damping in the solver does not add physical precision. At one further level, a Gaussian Motion multiplied by a Student-t Radio does not give a clean Student-t posterior, so a robust MAP fit is not an exact Student-t filter.

Comparing models: normalization constants matter

Within one model, constants that do not depend on the state do not affect the solution, so solvers drop them. Across models, however, these constants matter again. In 2D with scale Q, the Gaussian and every Student-t happen to share the same normalization,

CQ=log⁡(2π)+12log⁡∣Q∣,C_Q=\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|Q|,CQ​=log(2π)+21​log∣Q∣,

but the Gaussian with 2Q adds log⁡2\log2log2 per observation. A has 6 Radio observations, so this difference is 6log⁡2≈4.1588836\log2\approx4.1588836log2≈4.158883, and in the saved results it is enough to reverse the ranking:

Saved solution for snapshot ATotal objective without Radio constantsRadio constantsObjective with constants added back
Gaussian 2Q196.53426017.772921214.307181
Student-t, S=Q198.09243813.614038211.706477

The constants of the other factors are identical and are omitted. If the raw solver costs are compared, 2Q looks better, while if the densities are compared, the Student-t looks better. This comparison is still not Bayesian model evidence, because it evaluates each objective at its own selected parameters, without integrating over the parameter posterior and without any cost for complexity or selection.

The fair generalization test is to freeze the fit and compare normalized predictive densities on observations that did not take part in the construction:

p(znew∣D,M)=∫p(znew∣θ,M) p(θ∣D,M) dθ.p(z_{new}\mid D,M) =\int p(z_{new}\mid\theta,M)\,p(\theta\mid D,M)\,d\theta.p(znew​∣D,M)=∫p(znew​∣θ,M)p(θ∣D,M)dθ.

This test needs the full definition of the observations: choosing the one position island that a later algorithm selected and calling it “independent radio truth” changes the question. The post on frozen prediction discusses this topic.

Conclusion

A Student-t with S=QS=QS=Q is not “a Gaussian with Q and fatter tails”. It has twice the covariance, 1.5 times the central curvature, and a radial curvature that becomes negative beyond two whitened standard deviations, so matching any one of these properties leaves the others different. In the flat-floor walk, this combination helped dramatically where one observation conflicted strongly with the rest (A), changed little where no observation did (D), and made the result worse or failed to converge in the other snapshots (B, C).

The next round should first fix the matching condition and then freeze the scale, the initialization and the stopping rules for every model. Controls with equal covariance and with equal curvature should be separate arms that are registered in advance, and model selection should use new logs or held-out observations, not the same few sparse reference points.

Swapping a Gaussian for a Student-t changes the tail, the scale and the curvature at the same time; a good experiment states which of them it tests.

Within a snapshot all three likelihoods share this freedom, so local comparisons hold. Under a strict physical start, a common source and continuous updates, the gains need re-verifying.

Appendix B: related work on adaptive scale

Roth et al.’s analysis of exact Student-t recursions shows that once process and measurement noise are propagated, multiplied and conditioned, a simple recursion requires explicit approximation; their filter uses a joint t approximation with scale adjustment. That’s a different level from swapping the loss on a few position factors (paper, §3.3–4.3).

Deng et al. model both prediction error and measurement likelihood as Student-t, update scales with an inverse-Wishart prior and variational iterations, and model the selection process of whether an observation is transmitted. Their validation is a 2D constant-velocity simulation: 4D state, 2D position observations, process and measurement noise with 100× covariance at probabilities 5%/10%, 10,000 runs of 200 steps, 5 degrees of freedom. An RST-KF that knows the exact noise and receives every observation still beats the new method, so the information conditions differ (Sensors 2025, §4–5).

The most useful idea from that work is to separate three questions: does the tail shape fit, is the base scale miscalibrated, and do missing observations carry selection information? It doesn’t validate T31, and multimodal radio positions aren’t that simulation’s position measurements. Any adaptive-scale mechanism added later needs its own control, so the total gain doesn’t get credited to Student-t by default.
