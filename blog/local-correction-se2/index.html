Why a First-Order SE(2) Correction Stretches a Trajectory

A correction reduced the reference error from 24.5 m to 4.8 m but made the route 40% longer. This post shows why a large first-order rotation also scales, checks it on all 146 trajectory edges and fixes it by relinearizing.

Consider a correction from a flat-floor walking log (T31) that looks clearly successful: the mean horizontal error at four sparse reference points dropped from 24.51 m to 4.75 m. Should this correction be accepted?

The shape of the trajectory shows a problem. A displacement that measured 18.07 m before the correction measures 25.14 m after it, so the corrected route is closer to the reference points and about 40% longer than the walk that it should describe.

Both statements are true, because they measure different things. The distance to the reference points shows whether the trajectory ends up in the right place, while the motion between neighboring states shows whether the trajectory is still a plausible walk. This correction passed the first test and failed the second. The reason turns out to be one line of linear algebra: position was updated with I+θJI+\theta JI+θJ and attitude with a true rotation, and at nearly one radian these two updates no longer describe the same motion.

The post starts with the geometry of a first-order rotation, because this geometry already predicts the stretch. It then examines the walk, confirms the prediction on every edge of the trajectory, and shows how a “position-only” update ends up rotating the attitude. The last part presents the fix and its results.

Fundamentals: a rotation and its first-order impostor

Take an edge of length 10 that points along the x-axis, (10,0)(10,0)(10,0), and rotate it by 1 rad.

  • A true rotation gives about (5.403,8.415)(5.403,8.415)(5.403,8.415). The length is still 10.
  • The first-order update gives (10,10)(10,10)(10,10). The length is 10210\sqrt2102​, and the direction is only 45°.

The first-order point lies on the tangent line of the circle, not on the circle. For very small steps the difference is negligible, but when the first-order update is used as one large final update, both the length and the direction come out wrong.

Figure 1: Tangent vs. circle, drawn with case A’s perturbation angle and starting length. Pure-yaw approximation only; the tangent length isn’t a real trajectory end-point.

This observation can be made precise. A 2D rotation is

R(θ)=[cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ],R⊤R=I,R(\theta)= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \qquad R^\top R=I,R(θ)=[cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​],R⊤R=I,

so ∥R(θ)d∥=∥d∥\|R(\theta)d\|=\|d\|∥R(θ)d∥=∥d∥ for every ddd. Rigid motions can turn and translate a trajectory, but they cannot make a corridor larger. The first-order expansion around zero is

R(θ)≈I+θJ,J=[0−110].R(\theta)\approx I+\theta J,\qquad J=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.R(θ)≈I+θJ,J=[01​−10​].

Since J⊤=−JJ^\top=-JJ⊤=−J and J⊤J=IJ^\top J=IJ⊤J=I,

(I+θJ)⊤(I+θJ)=(1+θ2)I.(I+\theta J)^\top(I+\theta J) =(1+\theta^2)I.(I+θJ)⊤(I+θJ)=(1+θ2)I.

This identity gives a clean factorization of what the approximation actually does when it is applied as a finite transform:

∥(I+θJ)d∥=1+θ2 ∥d∥,I+θJ=1+θ2 R(arctan⁡θ).\|(I+\theta J)d\| =\sqrt{1+\theta^2}\,\|d\|, \qquad I+\theta J=\sqrt{1+\theta^2}\,R(\arctan\theta).∥(I+θJ)d∥=1+θ2​∥d∥,I+θJ=1+θ2​R(arctanθ).

So I+θJI+\theta JI+θJ is a rotation by arctan⁡θ\arctan\thetaarctanθ followed by a uniform scale of 1+θ2\sqrt{1+\theta^2}1+θ2​. The rotation is too small, and the scale is greater than one. For the perturbation in this case, θ=0.946055\theta=0.946055θ=0.946055 rad, the length multiplier is 1.376597 and the rotation is 43.412°. These two numbers will appear again in the data.

In 3D, with Ω=[ω]×\Omega=[\omega]_\timesΩ=[ω]×​, the same calculation gives

(I+Ω)⊤(I+Ω)=I−Ω2=(1+∥ω∥2)I−ωω⊤.(I+\Omega)^\top(I+\Omega) =I-\Omega^2 =(1+\|\omega\|^2)I-\omega\omega^\top.(I+Ω)⊤(I+Ω)=I−Ω2=(1+∥ω∥2)I−ωω⊤.

The component along the rotation axis is not stretched, while the perpendicular components are multiplied by 1+∥ω∥2\sqrt{1+\|\omega\|^2}1+∥ω∥2​. When the rotation is mostly about the vertical axis, as the yaw of a walking pedestrian is, the 2D result describes exactly what happens in the horizontal plane.

None of this is a flaw in Gauss–Newton, LM or Kalman filters. All of these methods run on local approximations, and local approximations work well locally. The failure here is specific: after one large step, the attitude has moved to a new linearization point while the position still sits on the old tangent.

The case: a correction that overshot in length

Case A from the flat-floor walk stores 147 states, 146 neighboring edges and 6 representative radio observations. Four surveyed reference points (POSI records in the log) are used only for evaluation, and two of them are at the same location, so they overlap on the plot.

The old solution did not rotate in the wrong direction, because the direction error of the outbound leg went from about −37.00° to 6.19°. It rotated the trajectory toward the right corridor and enlarged it at the same time. The relinearized solution, presented at the end of the post, brings the outbound leg close to the reference while it leaves the return leg slightly short.

Testing the hypothesis, edge by edge

The end-point distances show that the trajectory was deformed. To test whether a first-order rotation explains this particular deformation, compare every neighboring displacement before and after the correction:

di=pi+1−pi,di∗=pi+1∗−pi∗.d_i=p_{i+1}-p_i,\qquad d_i^*=p_{i+1}^*-p_i^*.di​=pi+1​−pi​,di∗​=pi+1∗​−pi∗​.

Because the common translation EaEaEa cancels in the difference, fitting di,EN∗≈Mdi,ENd_{i,EN}^*\approx M d_{i,EN}di,EN∗​≈Mdi,EN​ reveals the actual linear shape of the position update, without any influence from the internal translation parameter. For the saved coordinates of case A:

M=[1.002909−0.9527230.9398960.996521].M= \begin{bmatrix} 1.002909&-0.952723\\ 0.939896&0.996521 \end{bmatrix}.M=[1.0029090.939896​−0.9527230.996521​].

The singular values of this matrix show how much it stretches:

M=UΣV⊤,Σ=diag⁡(1.383731,  1.369400).M=U\Sigma V^\top,\qquad \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(1.383731,\;1.369400).M=UΣV⊤,Σ=diag(1.383731,1.369400).

Both directions are stretched by about 1.37–1.38, exactly where the theory predicted. The rotation part of the polar decomposition, UV⊤UV^\topUV⊤, comes out at 43.428°, against the 43.412° predicted from the world rotation increment. In addition, this single 2D map fits all 146 edges with a vector RMS residual of only about 7.003 mm.

The saved 3D computation for each state confirms the same point from a different direction. Here each neighboring edge is predicted from the world rotation increment of its own state:

  • with the first-order model, the RMS is about 0.006821 m;
  • with the same increment applied as a finite Exp, the RMS for predicting these old position edges is about 0.213481 m, roughly 31 times worse.

This result does not mean that finite rotations are less correct. It shows that the old position results were produced by a first-order map, so they are incompatible with an attitude that has already received a finite update. In other words, position and attitude now describe two different motions.

The 2D regression, the SVD, the polar decomposition and the end-point lengths were all recomputed from the saved coordinates, while the 3D residuals come from the original computation record. The core takes only a few lines:

before_edges = np.diff(before_positions[:, :2], axis=0)
after_edges = np.diff(linear_positions[:, :2], axis=0)
# One row per edge; transpose to get M in the column-vector convention.
row_map, _, rank, _ = np.linalg.lstsq(before_edges, after_edges, rcond=None)
M = row_map.T
U, scales, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M)
R_polar = U @ Vt
angle = np.arctan2(R_polar[1, 0], R_polar[0, 0])

The real code also checks that the 2D design matrix has full rank and compares the result against the saved matrix. This check matters, because on a perfectly straight trajectory the 2D map could not be identified.

How a position-only update ends up rotating attitude

This result raises a question. The old update path was called PURE_EN, because the radio factor observes only the horizontal position:

zi=pi,EN+ϵi.z_i=p_{i,EN}+\epsilon_i.zi​=pi,EN​+ϵi​.

The attitude Jacobian of this factor is zero. So where did a change of 43° in attitude come from?

It came from the rest of the graph. The state carries a joint prior over position, velocity and attitude, and the Motion factors connect neighboring states, so a position observation spreads through these correlations into every other part of the state. The old conditioning problem also had a common horizontal translation aaa without an independent prior. With the local perturbation δx\delta xδx, the common covariance PPP, the position selector HHH, the translation replicator AAA and the radio residual y=z−Hxy=z-Hxy=z−Hx, the linear-Gaussian objective is

min⁡δx,a  12δx⊤P−1δx+12(y−Hδx−Aa)⊤R−1(y−Hδx−Aa).\min_{\delta x,a}\; \frac12\delta x^\top P^{-1}\delta x+ \frac12(y-H\delta x-Aa)^\top R^{-1} (y-H\delta x-Aa).δx,amin​21​δx⊤P−1δx+21​(y−Hδx−Aa)⊤R−1(y−Hδx−Aa).

Eliminating δx\delta xδx gives:

S=HPH⊤+R,S=HPH^\top+R,S=HPH⊤+R, a=(A⊤S−1A)−1A⊤S−1y,δx=PH⊤S−1(y−Aa).a=(A^\top S^{-1}A)^{-1}A^\top S^{-1}y, \qquad \delta x=PH^\top S^{-1}(y-Aa).a=(A⊤S−1A)−1A⊤S−1y,δx=PH⊤S−1(y−Aa).

The implementation solves these equations with factorizations instead of explicit inverses. Everything important is in PH⊤PH^\topPH⊤: the observation selects only EN, but PH⊤PH^\topPH⊤ is nonzero in the rows for velocity and attitude. So “this update has no yaw parameter” and “yaw does not change in this update” are two different statements, and only the first statement was true.

The physical states are then formed as

pi∗=pi+δpi+Ea,vi∗=vi+δvi,Ri∗=RiExp⁡([δθi]×),p_i^*=p_i+\delta p_i+Ea,\qquad v_i^*=v_i+\delta v_i,\qquad R_i^*=R_i\operatorname{Exp}([\delta\theta_i]_\times),pi∗​=pi​+δpi​+Ea,vi∗​=vi​+δvi​,Ri∗​=Ri​Exp([δθi​]×​),

where EEE embeds the 2D translation in 3D. These formulas show the problem directly: position receives the additive first-order δpi\delta p_iδpi​, while attitude receives a finite Exp⁡\operatorname{Exp}Exp. The attitude δθi\delta\theta_iδθi​ is a local perturbation on the right side, so it must be converted before it can be compared with edges in the world frame.

📝 Further reading: For left vs. right perturbations, the exponential map and their Jacobians, Solà et al.’s micro Lie theory tutorial is the standard reference.1

The linear problem itself was solved correctly. For case A, an independent historical QR solution matches the original GLS translation to about 1.14×10−131.14\times10^{-13}1.14×10−13 m. The linear algebra is fine; the problem is that after a large step, the linear problem no longer describes the right geometry.

One more diagnostic trap exists. Here the internal translation aaa can reach tens of meters, and δpi\delta p_iδpi​ partly cancels it. So ∥a∥\|a\|∥a∥ alone shows neither the final displacement nor whether attitude and position agree, and only the full pi∗p_i^*pi∗​ and its edges show what happened.

Fixing it properly

The geometry must go back to the source of the state correlations: the Motion factors. The position and velocity residuals between neighbors are

rp,ij=Ri⊤(pj−pi)−Δpij,rv,ij=Ri⊤(vj−vi)−Δvij,r_{p,ij}=R_i^\top(p_j-p_i)-\Delta p_{ij}, \qquad r_{v,ij}=R_i^\top(v_j-v_i)-\Delta v_{ij},rp,ij​=Ri⊤​(pj​−pi​)−Δpij​,rv,ij​=Ri⊤​(vj​−vi​)−Δvij​,

and the attitude residual is built from the Lie-group log of the measured relative rotation against Ri⊤RjR_i^\top R_jRi⊤​Rj​. Together, these residuals require that position and velocity satisfy the relative motion again whenever the attitude changes. Keeping the quaternion normalized does not count as fixing the geometry.

The offline nonlinear program restored a boundary prior, the original Motion factors in the window and the selected Radio observations. In each iteration, the program evaluated the true residuals and Jacobians at the current state, applied the increment with a finite rotation, and accepted or rejected the step against the recomputed objective. Damping controls the step of the solver; it is not a physical rule such as “correct by at most N meters”.

Two details are easy to get wrong:

  • Do not count the same information twice. The full joint PPP already contains the Motion information, so it cannot be used as an extra prior while the original Motion factors are also added. Rebuilding the nonlinear problem requires knowing how the boundary prior and the internal factors decompose, and the final marginal covariance alone is not always enough for this.
  • A small step is not convergence. Heavy damping shrinks the steps even while the gradient is still large, so a small step can be a reason for stopping but is not automatically convergence. The historical solver records the objective, the gradient and the stopping reason, and all four results below are explicitly saved solutions with the original weights.

Can the same radio data explain an unstretched route?

Before the full nonlinear solve, the historical study ran a simpler sanity check. This check keeps the internal shape of the route, uses the same representative radio groups and the original QQQ, and fits only a finite rotation and a translation:

min⁡θ,t∑i(R(θ)pi+t−zi)⊤Qi−1(R(θ)pi+t−zi).\min_{\theta,t} \sum_i \bigl(R(\theta)p_i+t-z_i\bigr)^\top Q_i^{-1} \bigl(R(\theta)p_i+t-z_i\bigr).θ,tmin​i∑​(R(θ)pi​+t−zi​)⊤Qi−1​(R(θ)pi​+t−zi​).

The fit has no free scale, and POSI is not part of the objective. For a fixed θ\thetaθ the translation is linear, so θ\thetaθ can be searched over the full circle and cross-checked at the stationary points. The question is only whether the radio evidence requires a stretched route, and the answer is no: a rigid fit without stretching is compatible with the evidence. This rigid fit, however, treats the shape of the trajectory as known and drops the common uncertainty of the state, so it is a sanity check, not a navigation solution. The green curve in Figure 2 is the nonlinear Motion solution, not this rigid fit.

Why not just scale the output back?

A tempting shortcut is to divide by the known stretch of about 1.38. This shortcut does not work, for several reasons:

  • The scale comes from the rotation increment of one snapshot, and another snapshot has different state changes, observation support and common prior.
  • In 3D, the stretch depends on the direction, so there is no single number to divide by.
  • Rescaling the output breaks the relationships between position, velocity, attitude and the boundary prior. It restores neither the Motion residuals nor a correct covariance.

Clamping the internal translation does not help either. It might hide the distortion for a while, but it would also prevent the correction of genuine large drift. The fix belongs in the geometry and the solver of the problem, not in a clamp tuned to one case.

The radio QQQ was not changed and no heavy-tailed loss was introduced, so this improvement comes from the geometry and relinearization stage alone, separate from the later work with the Student-t distribution.

These results resolve the contradiction from the beginning of the post. The old linear solution really did get closer to the references. Relinearization with the same weights gets even closer and restores the main length relationships, so the error reduction no longer comes together with a stretched route.

Conclusion

When a first-order rotation is used as a large update, it acts as a smaller rotation plus a scale of 1+θ2\sqrt{1+\theta^2}1+θ2​; at almost one radian, this scale is a stretch of 38%, and all 146 edges of case A show exactly this stretch. A “position-only” radio factor still reaches the attitude through the joint prior and the Motion factors, so the attitude received a finite rotation while the position stayed on the tangent. Relinearizing the original nonlinear problem fixes the error, whereas scaling or clamping the output does not.

The checking habit from this case is simple. After accepting any large local increment, recompute the original nonlinear residuals and check that the finite attitude still agrees with the relative motion of position and velocity, because a small residual of the linear system only shows that the linear equations of this round were solved correctly. The edge-by-edge map shows what the update actually did to the trajectory.

Appendix: snapshots and recomputation

📝 Note: Paths in this appendix such as bench_mur/ and rep_mur/ are in my private research repository, so they aren’t public; they’re listed so each result can be traced.

The analysis uses four saved T31 snapshots. The trajectories, nonlinear solutions and 3D per-state audit are historical artifacts; the 2D regression, end-point errors and new sketches were recomputed locally without rerunning the sensor front end.

The working repository keeps a minimal snapshot without latitude/longitude, AP identifiers or raw sensor streams. Each case keeps only relative time, the commonly shifted local trajectory, sparse references, the needed matrices and the historical results, along with full SHA-256 hashes of the source files, historical algorithm identities and extraction notes.
